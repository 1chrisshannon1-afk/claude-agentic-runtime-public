# FTC Endorsements, Testimonials & Fake Reviews — 16 CFR Part 465 + the Endorsement Guides

Company-agnostic doctrine. This file is the compliance domain's canonical source for the two
distinct FTC frameworks a GTM seat runs into constantly: the 2024 **Trade Regulation Rule on the
Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials** (16 CFR Part 465, legally binding, penalty-bearing) and
the older **Endorsement Guides** (16 CFR Part 255, non-binding interpretive guidance the FTC uses to
signal how it will enforce Section 5 of the FTC Act against deceptive endorsement practices).
Every GTM seat that drafts review-acquisition programs, testimonial-based ad creative, influencer/
affiliate arrangements, or review-response copy reads this file first. See
[`review-gating.md`](review-gating.md) for the deep-dive on gating specifically (item 2 below), and
`reputation/never-do.md` in this same KB for the content-strategist seat's fast pre-publish
checklist, which cites this file's claims.

---

## 0. The two-framework distinction (read this before citing either one)

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-001]** 16 CFR Part 465, the Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials,
is a legally binding Trade Regulation Rule, not guidance — it took effect **October 21, 2024** and
authorizes civil penalties for a "knowing" violation, enforceable by both the FTC and (per the
Rule's own text) by suit for civil penalties under 15 U.S.C. § 57a. (official, FTC final rule
notice / eCFR Part 465 index page, retrieved 2026-09-27:
https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/part-465 — the FTC's own hosted eCFR text at ecfr.gov
returned a 403 to automated fetch on 2026-09-27; Cornell's Legal Information Institute mirror of
the same eCFR text is the cited source per this corpus's UNVERIFIED/mirror protocol.)

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-002]** 16 CFR Part 255 (the "Endorsement Guides") is the FTC's older, separate,
non-binding interpretive guidance on endorsements and testimonials generally — material-connection
disclosure, honest representation of actual experience, and disclosure placement/format. It predates
Part 465, was last substantively revised in 2023, and is enforced indirectly, through FTC Act
Section 5 unfair-or-deceptive-practices actions, not through its own standalone penalty schedule.
(official, FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ page, retrieved 2026-09-27:
https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking)

**Do not cite Part 255 when the fact pattern is actually a Part 465 violation (fake reviews, bought
reviews, review suppression, review gating) — this corpus's predecessor content file made exactly
this mistake once and corrected it** (see `reputation/never-do.md`'s own 2026-09-05
correction note: an earlier draft cited "Part 255" for the fake-review rule; the correct citation is
Part 465). Part 255 governs endorser/influencer disclosure; Part 465 governs the reviews-and-
testimonials ecosystem itself (who's writing them, whether they're real, whether they're being
gated or suppressed).

## 1. 16 CFR Part 465 — what it actually prohibits, section by section

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-003]** Part 465's operative sections, by structure (official, eCFR text via Cornell
LII mirror, retrieved 2026-09-27, https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/part-465):

| Section | Prohibits |
|---|---|
| § 465.2 | Fake or false consumer reviews, consumer testimonials, or celebrity testimonials — writing, selling, purchasing, or (for a business) procuring such a review/testimonial for its own products, knowing it misrepresents the reviewer's actual experience or that the reviewer doesn't exist. |
| § 465.3 | (Reserved in the current structure as retrieved — no operative prohibition text at this section as of retrieval; do not treat § 465.3 as carrying a claim.) |
| § 465.4 | Buying positive (or negative, of a competitor's product) consumer reviews. |
| § 465.5 | Insider consumer reviews and consumer testimonials — a company officer, employee, or someone materially connected to the business writing or disseminating a review/testimonial about that business's own product without clearly and conspicuously disclosing the connection, when the review is likely to affect consumers' purchasing decisions. |
| § 465.6 | Creating or controlling a website or entity that consumers would reasonably believe is an independent reviewer, when it's actually company-controlled. |
| § 465.7 | Review suppression — using unfounded or groundless legal threats, or intimidation, to prevent or remove a negative review; misrepresenting that all of a business's reviews are positive/negative when the business has actually suppressed some; suppressing a segment of reviews (e.g., all negative ones) in a way that distorts the overall star rating or review count displayed to consumers. |
| § 465.8 | Misuse of fake indicators of social media influence — buying or selling fake followers, views, or engagement, or using them to misrepresent a person's or business's importance for commercial purposes. |
| § 465.9 | Severability — if any part of the Rule is held invalid, the rest stays in force. |

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-004]** Penalty exposure: violations of Part 465 draw the FTC Act's standard civil
penalty ceiling under 15 U.S.C. § 45(m)(1)(A), which is inflation-adjusted annually and is **shared
across most FTC rule violations** (it is not a Part-465-specific number) — as of 2026-09-27 the
current ceiling is **$53,088 per violation**, unchanged from the January 17, 2025 adjustment because
the FTC froze the 2026 inflation adjustment (a government-shutdown data disruption meant no new
multiplier was published for 2026, so the 2025 figure carried forward under OMB Memorandum M-26-11).
(official, calculated from two FTC/Federal Register sources, retrieved 2026-09-27: the January 2025
adjustment from $51,744 to $53,088 — https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/02/ftc-publishes-inflation-adjusted-civil-penalty-amounts-2025
— and the 2026 freeze at that same figure, reported via
https://www.inkl.com/news/ftc-freezes-civil-penalty-amounts-for-2026-after-government-shutdown-disrupted-inflation-data,
a third-party report of the Federal Register notice — treat the freeze fact as `(third-party,
retrieved 2026-09-27)` pending direct confirmation from the Federal Register text itself, which was
not independently opened this session). **Each individual fake review, each individual suppressed
review, each individual bought review is very plausibly its own "violation"** — the per-violation
multiplication is what turns a small review-manipulation program into a large exposure number
quickly; this corpus does not assert a specific court has multiplied violations this way for a Part
465 case yet (the Rule is young), but the Rule's text and the FTC Act's general enforcement pattern
both support per-instance counting, and every downstream doctrine file in this KB treats it that way
as the conservative assumption.

## 2. What each § 465 prohibition means operationally for a GTM seat

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-005]** (official, § 465.2 text per claim 003's table above) § 465.2 (fake/false reviews) — never write a review as if from a customer
who doesn't exist or didn't have the experience described; never accept a vendor/agency offer to
"seed" reviews from fabricated accounts; never generate a review with an LLM and post it as a real
customer's words. **Reject example:** asking an intern or the founder's spouse to post a five-star
review under a made-up name. **Accept example:** the founder personally writing and posting a
review of their *own* experience with a *different, unrelated* business they genuinely patronized —
that's a real, first-party opinion, not a fake review, though § 465.5's insider-disclosure rule may
still apply if there's a material connection to disclose.

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-006]** § 465.4 (buying reviews) — never pay, or offer a discount/product/service
conditioned on a review being posted at all, even if the payment isn't conditioned on the review
being positive. The FTC's own guidance is explicit that even a *disclosed* incentive tied to
producing "5-star reviews" specifically is a violation — the disclosure defense that works for
ordinary sponsored-content endorsements (Part 255) does not cure a Part 465 bought-review violation,
because the harm the Rule targets is the review's authenticity signal being purchased, not just an
undisclosed relationship. (official, FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ, retrieved 2026-09-27, same source
as [KB-COMPLIANCE-002]: "paying for '5-star reviews' is a violation even with a disclosure
attached.") **Reject example:** "Leave us a Google review and get 10% off your next service."
**Accept example:** "We'd appreciate your honest feedback — here's a link to leave a review" with no
incentive of any kind attached, sent identically to every customer regardless of expected sentiment.

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-007]** (official, § 465.5 text per claim 003's table above) § 465.5 (insider reviews) — an owner, employee, or contractor with a
material financial connection to the business must clearly and conspicuously disclose that
connection if they review or testify about the business publicly. This is the same disclosure
discipline Part 255 requires for paid endorsers, applied specifically to reviews. **Reject example:**
an employee posting a five-star Google review of their own employer with no disclosure of employment.
**Accept example:** the same review with "(I work here)" or equivalent visible in the review text.

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-008]** (official, § 465.6 text per claim 003's table above) § 465.6 (company-controlled review sites) — never build or promote a
"review" or "comparison" site that looks independent but is secretly owned/controlled by the
business being reviewed favorably on it. This is a distinct risk from an ordinary owned-media
comparison page (e.g., "Us vs. Competitor X" on the company's own clearly-branded site) — the
violation is specifically about *disguising* the ownership so a consumer believes it's independent.

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-009]** (official, § 465.7 text per claim 003's table above) § 465.7 (review suppression) — never send a cease-and-desist, legal threat,
or intimidating message to a reviewer solely to get a negative-but-truthful review taken down; never
claim publicly ("we have all 5-star reviews," "100% satisfaction") when negative reviews exist and
have been hidden, filtered out of a widget, or otherwise kept from public view in a way that distorts
the aggregate rating shown to a prospective customer. This is distinct from (and does not prohibit)
legitimate content moderation — removing a review that violates a platform's own terms (spam, hate
speech, a review about the wrong business) is not suppression; removing or hiding a review *because
it is negative and true* is. See [`review-gating.md`](review-gating.md) for the closely related but
legally distinct gating violation (§ 465.7 is about hiding/removing reviews that already exist;
gating is about selectively soliciting so unhappy customers never get asked in the first place).

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-010]** (official, § 465.8 text per claim 003's table above) § 465.8 (fake social proof / fake followers) — never buy followers, likes,
views, or engagement, and never recommend a vendor or "growth service" that provides them, for any
GTM-managed social account. This includes fabricated engagement used to make an ad's social proof
(e.g., "10K happy customers") appear larger than reality.

## 3. Material connection disclosure (Part 255 doctrine, still binding via FTC Act Section 5)

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-011]** Any relationship between an endorser and the business — payment, free
product, an affiliate commission, an employment relationship, a family relationship — that "a
significant minority of consumers wouldn't expect" and that could affect how the audience weighs the
endorsement must be **clearly and conspicuously disclosed**, in the same medium as the endorsement
(a visual endorsement gets a visual disclosure; an audio endorsement gets an audible disclosure), and
placed where it will actually be seen — not buried in a bio, a "more" expansion, or a linked terms
page. (official, FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ, retrieved 2026-09-27, same source as
[KB-COMPLIANCE-002].) Acceptable short-form disclosures per the FTC's own examples: "#ad," "Paid
by [Brand]," "I received this free from [Company]."

**[KB-COMPLIANCE-012]** An endorsement must reflect "the accurate experience and opinion of the
endorser" — an agent drafting or ghost-writing testimonial copy on a customer's behalf must not
attribute experiences, results, or opinions the customer didn't actually report; a customer-quote
edited for length/clarity is fine, but a quote materially rewritten to claim a result the customer
never described is a fabricated testimonial, not an edited one, and falls back under the § 465.2
fake-review/testimonial prohibition above if it's being used in a review-shaped context, or under
ordinary FTC Act Section 5 deception doctrine if used as ad copy.

## 4. Working checklist before any review/testimonial-adjacent action ships

1. Is every review/testimonial attributed to a real person who actually said or experienced what's
   quoted? If no → stop, this is § 465.2.
2. Is anything — money, discount, product, service — being offered in exchange for leaving a review
   at all, or for a specific star rating? If yes → stop, this is § 465.4 (see
   [`review-gating.md`](review-gating.md) if the mechanism is selective solicitation rather than a
   direct incentive).
3. Does the person leaving the review/testimonial have a financial or employment connection to the
   business? If yes → disclosure required, § 465.5 / Part 255.
4. Is any review being hidden, filtered, or legally threatened into removal because it's negative and
   true? If yes → stop, this is § 465.7.
5. Is any follower/engagement count on a GTM-managed account inflated by purchase? If yes → stop,
   this is § 465.8.
6. Is a "comparison" or "review" site being built that isn't clearly, visibly owned by the business
   it favors? If yes → stop, this is § 465.6.

## 5. Contractor bend

A small owner-operated residential remodeling business almost never has a marketing department running a
sophisticated review-manipulation scheme on purpose — the real risk is the owner or a spouse
handling reviews personally reaching for something that feels like "just being efficient": asking
the crew lead to mention reviews only to customers who seemed happy on the final walkthrough (review
gating — see [`review-gating.md`](review-gating.md)), offering a small discount for "leaving us a
review" without separating that from star-rating conditioning, or asking an employee to post a
review of the family business without thinking of it as needing a disclosure. None of the legal
exposure changes for this buyer — the $53,088-per-violation ceiling applies identically regardless
of company size — but the messaging to this buyer should skip the CFR-section framing and lead with
the plain consequence: "if this gets reported, it's not a slap on the wrist, and Google can also
independently penalize your listing on top of any FTC action — ask every customer the same way,
every time, with nothing attached, and you're completely safe."
