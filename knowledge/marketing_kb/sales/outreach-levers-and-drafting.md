# Outreach Levers and Drafting Method

How to decide what an outreach or promotional message should lead with, how to make it relevant to a
defined group, and how to draft it with an AI assistant without shipping generic copy. It applies to
outreach a business writes to other businesses, partners, referral sources and prospects who have not just
asked for it. It is not a template for messages to the patients or prospective patients (leads) of a health
practice: such a message never leads with a guarantee, free service, discount or waived copay, and a reminder is kept apart from
any offer (the compliance lane decides). Reply-side work
is in `reply-handling.md`; touch counts and channel limits are in `follow-up-cadences.md`; consent and
the dormant-contact boundary are in `reactivation.md`. [KB-SALES-104]

**Evidence grade.** The method is distilled from one practitioner interview (published 2026-10-09,
retrieved 2026-10-11), a single self-reported source from inside a tool-vendor ecosystem. What the source
asserts about effectiveness is tagged `UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation` and its figures must never
be quoted as benchmarks. Rules this corpus adopts are tagged `(internal doctrine)`. Test a lever against
the business's own results before treating it as a rule for that business. The speaker and the interview's title are not named in this corpus; the grade rests on the source type, not on who said it. [KB-SALES-105]

## 1. The five levers

**[KB-SALES-082] UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation** A message can compete on five things, and a
working message leads with at least one: (1) the **offer**, including a guarantee the business can honor;
(2) a **unique mechanism**, the thing that separates this business from the alternatives; (3) a **free
value hook**, a slice of the service given away (a short report or a plan; a free clinical service in a regulated profession is cleared by the compliance lane first); (4) **segmentation**,
relevance to a defined group; (5) **social proof** the reader recognizes.

**[KB-SALES-083] (internal doctrine)** Choose the lead lever before writing any copy and state it at the
top of the plan. If no lever is defensible for the offer, say so and stop; do not compensate with volume.

**[KB-SALES-084] UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation** The source says surface details (subject-line
wording, call-to-action wording, signature layout) make no difference at all and, in an exchange the guest agreed
with, that two or three levers (offer and free value hook) carry most of the effect; neither is measured. **(internal doctrine)** Use this as a
testing priority order, not a ban: test levers first, surface details after, and run every test under
the conversion corpus's A/B testing discipline file (one variable, a confidence threshold, a minimum run time).

**[KB-SALES-085] (internal doctrine)** A guarantee, a free offer and a named customer are claims. Each
needs a documented basis the business can honor every time, and passes the claims checklist and the
compliance corpus (CAN-SPAM for email, TCPA for texts and calls, endorsement and advertising rules, and
any regulated-profession overlay for the tenant's industry) before use. A claim that would lift replies
but cannot be documented or honored is a defect. In a health practice a named customer is usually a
patient and needs a HIPAA authorization, and segmenting patients by the service they bought can count as
HIPAA marketing: check the compliance lane's HIPAA marketing rules before either. Offers must come from the approved company profile; if
it carries none, offer none.

**[KB-SALES-086] (internal doctrine)** Never offer anything of value in exchange for a referral, for
steering customers, or for a review. A free value hook is something useful in its own right to the reader
(information, a short assessment of their own situation), never a reward for sending business. Free or
discounted offers in a regulated profession can carry extra restrictions (for example on discounts for
insured customers); the compliance lane decides, this file does not.

## 2. Segment by one variable

**[KB-SALES-087] UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation** Pick one variable that changes what the reader
cares about (how they came to the business, time since the last contact, the service bought, the type or
size of business), sort every recipient into a segment on it, and write each segment its own angle on that
segment's pain. The test of a good segment message: it could not be sent unchanged to a different
segment. The source reports that its data vendor's AI step classified a demo list for about 11 cents (list size
not given); treat that cost as unverified.

**[KB-SALES-088] (internal doctrine)** The business owner or staff supplies the pain; the assistant does
not know the customers better than they do. The assistant is a good sorter and drafter, never the source of
the insight. Where the data is thin, the assistant states it is unsure instead of guessing.

**[KB-SALES-089] (internal doctrine)** If the sort shows a segment the offer does not fit, drop it or
write it a separate message. Do not cover the gap with generic copy.

**[KB-SALES-090] (internal doctrine)** Build segments only from data the business already holds
legitimately and may use for this purpose. Consent and opt-out status travel with each person (see
`reactivation.md` and `follow-up-cadences.md`).

## 3. Draft with the assistant, edit as a human

**[KB-SALES-091] (internal doctrine)** Give the assistant the segment, its pain, the lead lever, the tone
(short, direct, conversational) and the single action wanted. Ask for about four variants of the first
message; compare them in the reply and submit only the chosen one for approval. Take the best opener, body and call to action across variants, ask the assistant to fix the
joins, then read it as the recipient: cut anything generic, anything that could go to anyone, and any
claim the business cannot prove.

**[KB-SALES-092] UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation** The source says an assistant's unedited first
draft is probably better than a first-timer's (he puts it at about 80% of the time) and still edits every
draft. The human editing
pass is not optional.

**[KB-SALES-093] (internal doctrine)** A person approves before anything is sent. The drafter's own check never
replaces a separate reviewer: claims, identity and compliance are checked by a separate reviewer, and any edit after the
check is checked again.

**[KB-SALES-094] (internal doctrine)** Keep a swipe file of strong writing and learn its cadence (sentence
length, rhythm, where the point lands), not its words. Reuse structure, never text. Whoever edits the
assistant's draft needs basic copywriting judgment; reading a swipe file for rhythm is the cheapest way to
build it. `UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation` The source also recommends a short course or book.

## 4. Launch small, learn fast

**[KB-SALES-095] UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation** The source holds that an imperfect first send
teaches more than another round of drafting. **(internal doctrine)** Send an approved first message to a
small segment, read the replies and opt-outs, adjust the lead lever or the segment, send again. This never
waives approval, consent, opt-out or quiet-hours rules.

## Anti-patterns

- One generic message to the whole list.
- Testing button text and subject wording while the offer and list are unproven.
- A guarantee, free offer or named customer that is undocumented or not honored.
- Anything of value offered for a referral, for steering customers or for a review.
- Sending an unedited assistant draft, or an assistant checking its own draft.
- Copying another writer's sentences instead of their structure.
- Quoting the source's figures as benchmarks.
