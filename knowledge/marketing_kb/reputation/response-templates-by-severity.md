# Response Templates by Severity

How a review response changes by star rating, and the required structure for a negative-review
response. Every draft is proposed, never auto-posted — see `escalation-triggers.md` for when a
draft needs the operator's hand before it even gets written.

## 0. Mandatory pre-filter for a HIPAA-covered practice — read this before anything else in this file

**For any dental, medical, or other HIPAA-covered practice account, every reply first passes
`compliance/hipaa-marketing-for-practices.md` §6.1's non-confirming reply template — full stop,
before this file's severity-ladder structure below is applied.** §6.1 overrides two specific rules
in this file for a practice account: (a) §2's "reference one SPECIFIC detail from their review"
rule, and (b) §5's checklist item asking whether the draft references something specific from the
review — **neither applies to a practice reply**, because referencing a treatment, cost, or
insurance detail is exactly the PHI disclosure §6.1 bans, even when the reviewer volunteered it
first. §6.1 also governs the "thank them by first name" instruction in §2 below: never confirm or
imply patient status by name-and-relationship framing for a practice reply — see §6.1 rule 1's own
neutral-language requirement. See `escalation-triggers.md` §0 for the matching pre-filter on
whether a draft may be written at all.

## 1. The severity ladder (mechanism ported from prior dental-vertical reputation doctrine)

**Citation (internal doctrine, dental vertical, retrieved 2026-09-05):** *"For 4-5 star reviews:
draft a response within 24 hours. Address the reviewer by first name if provided. Reference a
specific detail from the review. Offer a genuine thank-you for their trust. For 1-3 star reviews:
flag for same-day operator attention before drafting. Never post a defensive or templated response.
Draft must be reviewed by the operator before submission."* The dental-vertical source treats 1-3 stars as
one bucket; this file splits 3 from 1-2 because a 3-star review reads very differently from a
1-star review (see §3) and the response structure genuinely differs — this is a refinement of the
mechanism, not a departure from it.

**No auto-posting, at any severity.** Every response — even a glowing 5-star reply — is a proposed
draft the operator approves before it goes live. **(official, Google Business Profile Help, "Read
& reply to reviews," retrieved 2026-09-28: https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474050):**
Google's own help page documents that an owner CAN edit or delete a reply after posting it ("Under
the reply you want to edit, select Edit... If you want to delete your reply, select Delete") — so
the mechanical edit/delete question is resolved, and a prior draft of this note was probably wrong
to leave it open. The safer framing still governs, independent of that mechanical fact: a published
reply is seen immediately, the reviewer is notified, and screenshots outlive any later edit or
deletion — so treat publication as effectively irreversible in PRACTICE regardless of the platform
technically allowing an edit/delete, and apply that same posture to every review platform unless
proven otherwise for a specific one.

## 2. 5-star and 4-star responses

**Timing:** within 24 hours (mechanism, dental-vertical doctrine).

**Structure:**
1. Thank the reviewer by first name if given.
2. Reference one SPECIFIC detail from their review — not a generic "thanks for your feedback."
   Specificity is what separates a real response from an obviously templated one.
3. Reinforce the relationship (invite them back, a brief "referrals are welcome" mention is fine)
   — but keep the referral ask itself short and secondary; a dedicated referral-doctrine file does
   not exist yet in this corpus (2026-09-05), so treat "how the ask is phrased" as a judgment call
   pending that doctrine being written, not as a solved question with a citation to lean on. Never
   cram a real referral pitch or incentive offer into a review reply itself — see `never-do.md` #1
   on why an incentive tied to a review outcome is a distinct, banned mechanism regardless.
4. Sign with a real name, not "The Team" or "Management."

**Contractor-bend example (internal-derived, no direct dental-copy port — a dental "thanks
for trusting us with your smile" line does not translate):**

> Thanks, [First Name] — really glad the guest bath turned out the way you pictured it. The tile
> work on that shower niche was one of our favorite details on the job too. If you ever need
> anything else around the house, or know someone else planning a remodel, we're always around.
> — [Real name], [Company]

**4-star handling:** same structure as 5-star. Do NOT ask "what would have made it 5 stars?" in the
public reply — that reads as fishing for a rating change, which several platforms (including
Google) treat as a policy-adjacent gray area (see `never-do.md` #2). If the review names a specific
gap, acknowledge it honestly in the reply; if it doesn't, thank them and move on.

## 3. 3-star responses (the "mixed" bucket)

**Timing:** same-day operator attention before drafting (mechanism, treated as the 1-3 star bucket
in the dental-vertical source; this file's split still keeps 3-star inside "needs operator eyes before a
draft goes anywhere," it just does not automatically treat it as an escalation-trigger review — see
`escalation-triggers.md` §3, the soft-escalation rule).

**Structure — a 3-star review typically has both a genuine positive and a genuine complaint. Answer
both, in that order:**
1. Acknowledge what went right (there is usually something — the review said 3 stars, not 1).
2. Address the specific complaint directly, without being defensive. Do not explain away the
   customer's experience or argue that they are wrong about what happened.
3. Offer a concrete next step (a phone number, an email, an invitation to discuss) — move the
   detailed back-and-forth OFF the public review thread.
4. Keep it short. A 3-star reply that turns into a paragraph of self-justification reads worse than
   the original review.

**Example:**

> Hi [First Name] — glad the new vanity and lighting worked out. Sounds like the punch-list items
> took longer to close out than they should have, and that's on us. Give us a call at [number] and
> we'll make sure everything on that list actually got finished. Appreciate you giving us the
> chance to make it right.

## 4. 1-star and 2-star responses (negative reviews)

**Timing:** flagged for same-day operator attention BEFORE any draft is written (mechanism, internal doctrine, dental vertical).
This is not a drafting-speed rule — it means the operator sees the raw review before an agent
writes anything, because a negative review can contain content that changes the whole response
strategy (a safety claim, a threat, a factual dispute about work performed) — see
`escalation-triggers.md`.

**Never post a defensive or templated response.** This is the single most-repeated instruction in
the dental-vertical source and it generalizes directly: a response that argues with the reviewer, minimizes
their experience, or reads as copy-pasted from a generic library does more reputational damage than
the original negative review.

**Required structure for a negative-review response:**
1. Acknowledge the specific issue named in the review — do not respond to a vague paraphrase of it.
2. Do not argue publicly about facts in dispute (what was actually said, done, or charged) — that
   argument belongs in a private conversation, never in the public reply thread.
3. Take responsibility for whatever part of the outcome is genuinely on the business, even if only
   partial responsibility.
4. Offer a specific, concrete resolution path (a name and phone number to call, not "please reach
   out to us") — and mean it; the reviewer or a later prospect reading the thread will notice a
   resolution offer that goes nowhere.
5. Close professionally. Do not end on a defensive note even if the body of the reply corrected a
   factual error.

**Example (a legitimate complaint, no legal/safety content):**

> [First Name], I'm sorry the tile delay pushed your timeline out as far as it did — that's a
> fair complaint and it's on us for not communicating it sooner. Please call me directly at
> [number] so we can talk through what happened and make it right. — [Real name], owner

**What NOT to write (reject examples):**
- "We're sorry you feel that way." (Non-apology; implies the complaint is about the reviewer's
  feelings, not the business's actual performance.)
- "This is not an accurate account of what happened." (Public factual dispute — see #2 above.)
- "We have never had a complaint like this before." (Defensive, unverifiable, and reads as calling
  the reviewer a liar.)
- Any reply that ignores the specific complaint and pivots straight to "call us" with no
  acknowledgment at all — feels dismissive even though the CTA itself is correct.

## 5. Pass/reject gate for any drafted response, any severity

Before a drafted response is proposed to the operator, check it against:

- [ ] Does it reference something SPECIFIC from the actual review (not a generic template line)?
      **Does not apply to a HIPAA-covered practice reply — see §0 above; referencing a specific
      clinical/financial/insurance detail is exactly what §6.1 of the compliance file bans.**
- [ ] Is it free of any public factual dispute about what happened on the job?
- [ ] Is it free of any defensive or minimizing language ("we're sorry you feel," "that's not
      accurate," "first time we've heard this")?
- [ ] Does it move detailed resolution OFF the public thread (phone/email), for anything below
      4 stars?
- [ ] Is it signed by a real name?
- [ ] Has it cleared `escalation-triggers.md` — i.e., does this review actually contain content an
      agent may draft against, or does it need the operator's hand on the drafting itself?

**Pass example:** a 2-star review about a scheduling delay, answered per §4's structure, signed,
phone number offered. **Reject example:** the same review answered with "We apologize for any
inconvenience this may have caused" and nothing else — passes no specificity check, offers no
resolution path, reads as an auto-response.

## 6. Contractor bend

A dental practice's reviewer and a GC's reviewer are both real customers, but the STAKES per review
differ sharply for this buyer: the owner-operator residential-remodeling GC archetype (whose
relevant end-customer persona is `shared/homeowner-buyer-psychology.md`;
`shared/contractor-buyer-psychology.md` is a different, B2B software-buyer persona) often depends on a small
handful of recent reviews to win the NEXT job in a specific neighborhood, and negative reviews about
construction work carry concrete, checkable claims (a leak, a missed deadline, a change-order
dispute) that are much easier for a prospect to verify than a vague dental-service complaint. That
raises the bar on §4's "do not argue publicly about facts in dispute" rule — resist the urge to
defend craftsmanship publicly even when the reviewer is plainly wrong about a technical detail (they
call something "shoddy tile work" that was actually built to spec); the public reply is not the
place to win that argument, the phone call is. This buyer also personally reads and often personally
answers reviews already — a proposed draft in this seat's voice should sound like something a
busy owner-operator would actually type from a truck between jobs, not a corporate reputation-
management department. Keep replies short; a GC who reads three long, polished paragraphs and
thinks "I would never write like this" won't post it.
