# Voice and Vocabulary

**This is the voice module for B2B products sold to contractors/trades.** It applies only when a
company's marketing pack selects this persona — a company selling to a different buyer (e.g. a
homeowner-facing remodeler, or a non-trades vertical) should reach for a sibling persona file
instead (e.g. `../shared/contractor-buyer-psychology.md` vs. `../shared/homeowner-buyer-psychology.md`).
Read this file before writing customer-facing copy for a trades-facing brand; a company pack
selecting a different persona reads that persona file instead.

What words a trades-facing brand uses, what words it avoids, and why.

## 1. The one sanctioned speed claim

Use whatever exact, measured, hedged speed phrasing the current company has actually verified for
its own product (e.g. "Typically [measured range] from job description to estimate.") — the same
exact phrasing everywhere the speed claim appears, never a looser paraphrase.

**Banned:** "20 minutes," "instant," "in seconds," "in minutes" (unqualified), or any other
unmeasured time figure. See `ANTI-PATTERNS.md` #1 for the mechanism and the recurring live-copy
violation this class of claim tends to produce.

**Citation:** re-derive the current company's own sanctioned speed phrasing from its own
positioning doc — do not reuse a prior company's exact number for a different company's product.

## 2. Segment the pitch by audience, not by product

For a company selling into a market with a clear small-operator vs. growing-operation split
(common in home-services/trades verticals), two pitches typically apply to the same underlying
product:

1. **Solo operator → TIME.** The speed claim (see #1) is the whole pitch — this buyer wants their
   evening back.
2. **Growing operation (subs, PM/lead handoffs) → TRUST.** The line-item output becomes a bill of
   materials and work orders — an operations tool, not a proposal printer.

Both pitches run on the same engine. Never write copy that pretends these are different products;
segment the *emphasis*, not the substance.

**Citation:** re-derive the current company's own audience-segmentation framing from its own
positioning doc; the pattern above ("time" vs. "trust" split by operator size) is portable doctrine
for this vertical shape, not a fact about any one company.

## 3. Contractor vocabulary — words this buyer actually uses

A working residential-trades GC does not talk like a SaaS marketer. Words to reach for and
words to avoid:

| Use this | Not this | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "job," "bid," "the number" | "opportunity," "deal," "pipeline" | Sales-jargon words read as someone who has never bid a job. |
| "line items," "scope," "takeoff" | "deliverables," "output," "artifacts" | These are the trade's own vocabulary — CPQ/SaaS jargon signals an outsider. |
| "sub," "trade," "crew" | "vendor," "partner," "resource" | Contractors say sub or trade. "Vendor" and "resource" are corporate-procurement words. |
| "customer," "homeowner" | "client," "user," "end user" | "User" especially reads as software-company language, not construction language. |
| "estimate," "proposal" | "quote" (used loosely) inconsistently, "engagement" | Estimate and proposal are the trade's real terms; keep them distinct (estimate = internal number-building, proposal = what the customer sees), don't blur with "quote" unless matching the reader's own word. |
| "work order" | "task," "ticket," "action item" | Work order is a real construction document type — use it if the product exports one; "ticket" is a SaaS support-desk word. |
| "phone," "call," "text" | "channel," "touchpoint," "outreach" | This buyer is phone-first, in a truck. Marketing-analytics words like "touchpoint" don't belong in copy aimed at them. |

## 4. Tone

- **Short sentences. No adjectives that don't carry information.** Direct-response doctrine ("all
  facts, no adjectives, all specifics" — see `headline-doctrine.md`) applies doubly here: this
  buyer skims on a phone between jobs.
- **Specific over generic.** A real, sourced dollar figure and scope ("the proposal that closed a
  $[real sourced figure] [job type]," if real and sourced for the current company) beats "a large
  project." A named trade beats "your business."
- **No em-dashes in drafted customer-facing copy** where the current company's own style guide
  bans them (internal doctrine, mechanism: "no em-dashes in any customer-facing output — use
  hyphens" is a common house-style rule; apply it as a style preference for that surface, not a
  hard technical constraint, when the company's own internal writing uses em-dashes freely
  elsewhere).
- **Never talk down.** This buyer runs a real, established small-to-mid-size operation. They are
  not unsophisticated — they are time-poor and skeptical of software specifically, not skeptical of
  running a business.

## 5. What NOT to sound like

- Not a demo-ware AI startup ("magic," "revolutionary," "game-changing").
- Not an enterprise CPQ vendor (no "digital transformation," "workflow orchestration,"
  "synergies").
- Not a SaaS growth-hacking newsletter ("10x your pipeline," "unlock").
- Not defensive or hedge-everything corporate legal voice — hedge the specific claims that need
  it (speed, accuracy), not the whole voice.

## Contractor bend

Most brand-voice doctrine assumes a reader who trusts software vendors by default and needs to be
excited, not reassured. This buyer is the opposite: phone-first, in a truck, burned by software
before (a CRM that never got used, an accounting-software integration that broke, an "AI" tool that
produced garbage). Every piece of copy for this seat should read like it was written by someone who
has actually stood in a gutted bathroom with a tape measure — not by someone who has only ever
described one in a pitch deck. When in doubt, cut the adjective and add the specific: prefer a
concrete mechanism claim ("[the company's concrete mechanism claim], not an AI guess" — pull the
current company's own equivalent from its positioning doc rather than inventing one here) over a
generic superlative like "powerful estimating."
