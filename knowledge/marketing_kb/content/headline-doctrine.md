# Headline Doctrine

Headline and page-copy formulas, adapted from general direct-response copywriting doctrine for a
phone-first, software-skeptical contractor buyer. **This file is written for companies selling to
contractors/trades; adapt the buyer framing per the company pack's selected persona file** (see
`../shared/` for the available buyer-psychology persona files) when a company pack selects a
different buyer.

## 1. The core direct-response principle: specifics beat adjectives

**External doctrine (third-party, retrieved 2026-09-05):** classic direct-response copywriting
practice states a strong ad is "all facts, no adjectives, all specifics" — headlines that make a
concrete promise, address a real pain point, or use "you" statements outperform vague claims.
Sources: Mirasee's direct-response copywriting guide, HubSpot's direct-response copywriting tips
(both retrieved 2026-09-05, general marketing doctrine, not home-services-specific).

**What this means concretely:**
- REJECT: "Smarter estimating for smarter contractors." (adjective, no fact)
- ACCEPT: "Typically [measured range] from job description to estimate." (fact, specific,
  measured — see `voice-and-vocabulary.md` #1 for why the exact, current-company-verified phrasing
  is mandatory; re-derive the real figure per company)

## 2. Formula: AIDA / AIDCA, adapted

**External doctrine (third-party, retrieved 2026-09-05):** AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire,
Action) and its extension AIDCA (adding Conviction) are the standard direct-response structuring
formulas — grab attention, build interest, create desire, add conviction (proof), drive action.

**Adaptation for this buyer:**
1. **Attention** — name the actual pain: the evening lost to a spreadsheet, the bid that took too
   long and lost the job anyway, the "estimate" that was really a guess.
2. **Interest** — the mechanism, not the magic: a concrete, verifiable mechanism claim (the trust
   half of the pitch; pull the current company's own equivalent — see `voice-and-vocabulary.md` #2).
3. **Desire** — segment by audience: TIME for the solo operator, TRUST (bill of materials / work
   orders for subs) for the growing operation.
4. **Conviction** — proof, and only real proof: a sourced customer story (see
   `ANTI-PATTERNS.md` #11 on unverified testimonial figures), or the mechanism claim itself stated
   plainly (never a fabricated stat).
5. **Action** — one CTA, not three. See `email-sequence-doctrine.md` for CTA-clarity rules that
   apply equally to page copy.

## 3. Headline patterns that work for this buyer, with pass/reject examples

| Pattern | Pass example | Reject example | Why the reject fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| Name the pain, not the product | "Another bathroom to bid, another evening lost to a spreadsheet." | "Introducing the future of estimating." | Vague, product-centric, no reader pain named. |
| Measured claim, hedged honestly | "Typically [measured range] from job description to estimate." (re-derive the real, current figure per company) | "Estimates in 20 minutes." | Unmeasured figure — see `ANTI-PATTERNS.md` #1. |
| Mechanism over magic | "[The company's concrete mechanism claim] — not an AI guess." (pull the current company's own mechanism claim) | "AI that just knows your job." | Implies an unproven capability — check the current company's never-claim list, see `ANTI-PATTERNS.md` #2. |
| Trade vocabulary | "Turn your estimate into a work order your sub can act on." | "Turn your output into a deliverable your resource can action." | SaaS jargon — see `voice-and-vocabulary.md` #3. |
| Specific dollar/scope proof (only if real) | "The proposal that closed a $[real sourced figure] [job type]." (only ever with a real, sourced figure for the current company — verify sourcing per `ANTI-PATTERNS.md` #11) | "Estimates that win more jobs." | Generic outcome claim with no specific — direct-response doctrine calls this weak copy. |

## 4. The pricing-page lead-in (for money-moment products)

For a product that sits on a real money moment (a job won or saved from a bad bid), every pricing
section — static site and in-app — should carry a "pays for itself" frame as its standing lead-in,
never buried in a single variant.

**Citation:** re-derive the current company's own value-frame language from its positioning doc.
**(internal doctrine — re-derive flag):** tools that sit on a category's money moment (e.g. a
cost-estimation tool for a trade) tend to command real recurring pricing — anecdotally in the range
of a category-standard cost-estimation tool's published tier (e.g. roughly $2-3K/yr for an
established competitor in a given category, UNVERIFIED — re-derive the actual current external
precedent's published price from that vendor's own public pricing page before citing a number) —
because buyers judge them against the job they help win or protect, not against a generic
SaaS-utility bar. This is a portable value-framing pattern, not a specific company's price point;
never plug in this placeholder number as if it were verified, and never treat it as the current
company's own price.

- REJECT (frame it as a subscription cost): "Only [price]/month for unlimited estimating power." —
  deliberately price-agnostic here: the defect is the *subscription-cost framing itself* (any
  dollar figure plugged into this pattern reads as SaaS-line-item thinking), never a statement
  about what the correct price is. Do not plug in a real tier price when illustrating this reject
  pattern — this file is not the place a company's real price lives; get that from the current
  company's own pricing source of truth, and reusing a real number here would risk a future reader
  misreading a REJECT example as a comment on the price rather than the frame.
- ACCEPT (frame it as a revenue moment): "Pays for itself the first job it helps you win or save
  from a bad bid."

## 5. Never open with a claim from the anti-patterns list

Before shipping any headline or hero line, check it against `ANTI-PATTERNS.md` in full. The
fastest way a headline seat introduces a defect is a punchy line that happens to cross a
never-claim boundary (speed, photo-measurement, accuracy guarantee, unshipped feature).

## Contractor bend

Standard direct-response doctrine was built for readers deciding whether to buy something
discretionary — a course, a gadget, a service. This buyer is deciding whether to trust software
with the number that determines whether they make money on a job. The stakes framing is different:
lead with the mechanism and the honest hedge, not with hype. A headline that would work great for
a course-selling funnel ("This one trick changed everything") reads as exactly the kind of
overclaim this buyer has learned to distrust. When choosing between a punchier headline and a more
honest one, the honest one is the better direct-response choice for this specific audience — not a
compromise, the actual optimization target.
