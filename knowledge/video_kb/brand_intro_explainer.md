# Brand Intro & How-It-Works Explainer Videos (B2B Software, 60-120s)

Terse, applied rules for a worker agent building a brand-intro or how-it-works explainer for a
B2B software product. Every hard constraint below is a cross-company operator ruling or the
company's own vocabulary/claims list, and is non-negotiable — never trade it off against a "best
practice" from a source.

**Provenance:** aggregated from third-party creator-tool, vendor, and practitioner guides unless a source is marked OFFICIAL (published by the platform, standards body, regulator, or licensor itself). Every non-OFFICIAL source in this file is practitioner opinion, not platform documentation; any platform-behaviour claim (a platform limit, cap, spec, ranking behaviour, or policy) that rests only on such a source is UNCONFIRMED-PRIMARY (the `INDEX.md` flag set) until a platform page confirms it — never report it as the platform's own rule.

## Hard constraints (never contradict these)

- B-roll: at most 2.5 seconds per shot, no exceptions.
- B-roll: at most 20% of total runtime across the whole video.
- B-roll: never two consecutive b-roll shots back to back — always an a-roll/product/UI beat
  between any two b-roll shots.
- B-roll: none may start before the 3.0-second mark of the video.
- Open product-first — the product/UI is on screen before any brand story, logo animation, or
  founder framing.
- The company's vocabulary and claims list applies to narration, on-screen text, and all
  copy-facing text — vocabulary and claims are per company, not universal: banned terms, numeric-claim restrictions,
  and any other wording rule: follow the company pack's `claims_policy` and that company's own
  video-producer dossier; where neither states them, DEGRADED — never invent, assume, or carry over another company's list
  (see `INDEX.md`'s precedence note). Examples of what such a list may
  contain: banned jargon, or a no-numeric-time/accuracy-claims rule ("save 10 hours," "99%
  accurate").
- Viewing review: the rendered cut is watched — contact sheets against the timed transcript, per shot (primer §4/§7) — before any upload; the seat's own frame-viewing review satisfies this, no separate human viewer is required. It covers the assembled cut start to finish and happens before any
  upload to any channel — never skip this step, never upload sight-unseen.
- These rules override every stylistic recommendation elsewhere in this file. If a technique
  below would require breaking one, drop the technique instead.

## What this video type is for (brand-intro vs. how-it-works vs. demo)

- Brand-intro sells the "why" — it builds affinity and trust so a stranger cares who you are;
  emotional register, narrative framing. A product demo sells the "how" — functionality, for
  someone who already knows the brand and is evaluating fit. A how-it-works explainer sits
  between the two: it earns attention like a brand piece but resolves like a demo. [B2] [B7]
- Don't blend all three goals into one video. Pick brand-intro (make a stranger care) or
  how-it-works (make an interested viewer understand the mechanism) as the single job for this
  cut. A single video trying to be both a manifesto and a full feature tour dilutes both. [B2]
- Best-performing pattern across sources: brand pieces inspire, demos convert. If the brief needs
  both jobs, that is two videos, not one longer one. [B2]
- For a first-time visitor (homepage, cold social), default to the conceptual/brand-intro register
  at 60-75s. For someone already evaluating (post-signup, sales-assisted), lean how-it-works and
  allow up to ~90-120s. [B1]

## Structure and beats (90-second reference arc)

- Canonical timing arc from B2B practice: hook (0-5s) -> problem (5-20s) -> solution (20-45s) ->
  feature/mechanism walkthrough (45-70s) -> proof (70-80s) -> CTA (80-90s). Scale proportionally
  for 60s or 120s versions rather than inventing a new shape. [B1]
- Hook (first 3-10s) decides whether the rest gets watched. Buyers decide within ~10 seconds
  whether a demo/explainer is worth continuing; the first 3 seconds decide whether they keep
  scrolling at all. Treat the hook as its own deliverable, scripted and reviewed separately from
  the body. [B4]
- Two hook patterns that need no numeric claim (and so survive a company's no-numeric-claims rule):
  - Problem-recognition hook: name the specific frustration the viewer has muttered at their own
    screen ("still doing X"). No stat required. [B4]
  - Show-don't-tell hook: open on the product already doing something impressive, no narration
    setup, no logo bumper first. [B3]
- Never open with: logo animation, "Hi, we're building X," a founder introduction, or a slow
  fade-in tagline. All of these are dead time before the viewer has any reason to care. [B3]
- Problem beat: state the frustration plainly, in the viewer's own words, not the company's. One
  problem, not a list. Cutting a laundry list of pain points is a common failure mode — pick the
  single sharpest one. [B1] [B6]
- Solution/mechanism beat: show the shortest path from problem to outcome. This is where the
  "how it works" content lives — but keep it to the outcome-relevant mechanism, not a full
  feature inventory. Cut anything that slows the explanation down. [B1]
- Feature/walkthrough beat (how-it-works cuts only): real UI, real product, doing one coherent
  task end to end rather than a jump-cut feature list. Genuine screen capture reads as more
  credible than an animated approximation of the UI, especially for a technical or skeptical
  audience. [B3]
- Proof beat: one social-proof element (testimonial line, recognizable use-case, before/after
  framing) placed late but before the CTA, never buried at the very bottom with no lead-in.
  Video-format testimonials substantially outperform text for trust; even a single authentic line
  read in the target voice is worth the seconds. [B10]
- CTA beat: one primary ask only. Reinforce, don't stack — if the hook or body already implied an
  action, the end card restates the SAME ask rather than introducing a second one. [B5]
- Optional CTA reinforcement mid-video is legitimate (a soft on-screen prompt around the midpoint)
  as long as it doesn't compete with the end-card ask — same destination, same verb. [B5]

## Hooks and product-first opens

- "Product-first" means the product or its real UI is the first thing on screen doing something,
  not a metaphor, not a stock-footage cold open, not a narrator setting up context first. [B3]
- A cold open on the product already mid-task (not a static screenshot) reads as confident and
  respects the viewer's time. [B3]
- Match the open to the audience: a power-user/technical viewer is more persuaded by authentic UI
  in motion than by a polished animated abstraction of it. [B3]
- If the product itself isn't visually strong enough to open on (too plain, mid-setup state),
  open on the problem stated in plain language instead — never on a brand bumper as a filler
  default. [B3] [B4]
- Whatever the hook shows, it must pass the "is this relevant to me" test in 3 seconds — a
  generic "welcome to our platform" opener fails this regardless of production quality. [B4]

## Voice, tone, and script writing

- Write the way a person talks, not the way a company writes. Use contractions ("don't," "it's").
  Scripts that sound formal or corporate create distance from the viewer. [B9]
- Avoid buzzwords and vague superlatives ("cutting-edge," "innovative," "seamless," "next-gen") —
  they dilute meaning and read as untrustworthy. Prefer plain, specific language. [B9]
- Read every script line aloud before finalizing — stiffness and awkward phrasing show up in the
  ear long before they show up on the page. [B9]
- For a trade/contractor audience specifically: plain, direct language and real work/real people
  build trust faster than polish. Avoid any language that reads as a tech-industry pitch to a
  non-technical, on-the-job viewer. This is a stronger constraint for this audience than for a
  general SaaS buyer. [B8]
- One idea per sentence. A viewer hearing narration once (no re-reading, no pause button in
  practice) cannot hold a compound clause — split it.
- Never explain the same point twice in different words "for emphasis" — it reads as padding
  once written down, even though it can feel natural when talked through live.
- Say what the product does in terms of the outcome the viewer gets, not the mechanism producing
  it — this is also how a company's banned-jargon list usually gets naturally avoided: such words
  tend to describe mechanism, and mechanism-talk is exactly what a plain-language, outcome-first
  script doesn't need.

## Pacing, timing, and voiceover math

- Standard voiceover pacing for explainer content: 120-150 words per minute (WPM); 130 WPM is a
  reasonable conversational default. Complex/technical content should sit toward the slow end
  (~100-120 WPM); energetic/punchy delivery can run up to ~160 WPM but risks clarity. [B6] [B9]
- Rule-of-thumb word budgets at ~130-150 WPM: a 60s video ≈ 130-150 words of narration; a 90s
  video ≈ 195-225 words; a 120s video ≈ 260-300 words. Always verify against a script-timer tool
  or by reading the draft aloud with a stopwatch — don't trust the word count alone, since real
  delivery includes pauses. [B9]
- Silence and pauses are part of the pacing budget too — a script timed at exactly 90 seconds of
  continuous speech will run long once natural breath pauses and beat-changes are added. Budget
  ~10-15% slack.
- Retention data: viewer retention stays above ~70% under 60 seconds; by 2 minutes it drops to
  roughly 50%; abandonment accelerates sharply past the 2-minute mark. Landing-page conversion
  peaks for videos under 90 seconds, with measurable drop-off for every ~30 seconds beyond that.
  Treat 90 seconds as the practical ceiling for this format; 120 seconds is the edge, not the
  target. [B6]
- The same script can perform roughly 2x better trimmed to ~78s versus left at ~140s — cutting
  length is not just a nice-to-have, it measurably changes outcomes. Default to the shorter cut
  whenever the content survives the trim. [B6]

## B-roll usage (general craft, subordinate to the hard constraints above)

- General industry range for b-roll share of runtime across video types is 20-40% — this KB's
  hard ceiling of 20% (see Hard Constraints) sits at the conservative/low end of that general
  range on purpose; do not let a general source justify pushing toward 40%. [B7]
- Common general-purpose cadence is a b-roll cutaway every 20-40 seconds of runtime — for a
  60-120s video under this KB's 2.5s-per-shot / 20%-of-runtime ceiling, that generally works out
  to a small handful of short b-roll insert shots total, not a continuous b-roll montage. Plan
  b-roll as discrete punctuation, never as a sustained visual layer. [B7]
- Use b-roll to punctuate a beat change (problem -> solution, or one feature -> the next), not to
  fill time while narration continues on an unrelated line. Every b-roll shot should illustrate
  the sentence being spoken at that moment.
- Because b-roll can never start before 3.0s and can never run 2 shots consecutively, storyboard
  b-roll placements explicitly against the script timeline before recording/sourcing — don't
  place b-roll ad hoc in the edit and check the constraints after.

## End cards and CTAs

- One primary CTA per video. Everything else on the end card supports that single ask; competing
  asks ("sign up" + "watch another video" + "follow us") measurably weaken conversion. [B5]
- End-card placement converts meaningfully better than mid-roll for a product/demo-style video;
  reserve mid-roll prompts for educational/how-to content where the viewer may act mid-video. For
  this format, put the CTA at the end, optionally reinforced once earlier without introducing a
  second ask. [B5]
- A CTA should pass a "deletion test": strip words until removing one more changes the meaning.
  "Get started" beats "Click here to sign up for your account today." Keep the end-card line to
  the shortest version that still says exactly what happens next. [B5]
- Stack the CTA across channels available to the format: a spoken line + on-screen text + (where
  the platform supports it) a native click target — never rely on a single one of these alone.
  [B5]
- Where the company's claims list forbids numeric claims, never let the end card imply a numeric
  outcome ("get results in X days") — the very last thing the viewer sees is exactly where it's
  most tempting to sneak one in.

## Social proof and trust signals

- Video-format testimonials outperform text testimonials substantially for trust and conversion;
  even one short, specific customer line is disproportionately valuable versus none. [B10]
- Place proof/trust content late in the video but before the CTA — right before the ask, not
  buried at the very end after the ask has already been made, and not isolated at the very top
  before the viewer has any context for who is talking. [B10]
- Generic trust badges or vague "trusted by companies like yours" framing carry far less weight
  than one specific, verifiable, named example. Prefer specificity over volume.
- For a trade/blue-collar-facing product specifically, real people and real work read as more
  trustworthy than polished stock talent — favor an authentic voice/likeness over a studio-perfect
  one when the audience is on-the-job tradespeople rather than office-based SaaS buyers. [B8]

## Format checklist for a worker agent (apply in order)

1. Confirm the video's single job: brand-intro (make a stranger care) or how-it-works (make an
   interested viewer understand the mechanism) — not both.
2. Pick target runtime (60-90s default; 120s only if the content genuinely needs the room) and
   compute the narration word budget at ~130-150 WPM before writing the script.
3. Draft the hook first, separately, and test it alone against the "relevant to me in 3 seconds"
   bar before writing the rest.
4. Write the script in plain, spoken, outcome-first language; read it aloud; cut anything that
   sounds like a pitch deck.
5. Scan the draft against the company's vocabulary/claims list (pack `claims_policy` or dossier)
   — remove every banned term and every restricted claim.
6. Storyboard b-roll placements against the script timeline, checking each shot against all four
   b-roll hard constraints before recording/sourcing anything.
7. Confirm the open is product-first — no logo bumper, no founder-framing, before the product/UI
   appears on screen.
8. Confirm one proof element sits late, before the CTA.
9. Confirm the end card carries exactly one CTA, passes the deletion test, and contains no claim
   the company's list restricts.
10. Assemble the cut, then perform a full start-to-finish viewing review before any upload —
    never upload without this step, regardless of how confident the assembly looked on paper.
