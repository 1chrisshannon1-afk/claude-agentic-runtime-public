# Testimonial, Case Study & Before/After Video — Working Rules

Terse, applicable rules for producing customer testimonial, case study, and before/after "results"
videos. Read alongside the hard constraints below before writing any script, shot list, or consent
paperwork.

**Provenance:** aggregated from third-party creator-tool, vendor, and practitioner guides unless a source is marked OFFICIAL (published by the platform, standards body, regulator, or licensor itself). Every non-OFFICIAL source in this file is practitioner opinion, not platform documentation; any platform-behaviour claim (a platform limit, cap, spec, ranking behaviour, or policy) that rests only on such a source is UNCONFIRMED-PRIMARY (the `INDEX.md` flag set) until a platform page confirms it — never report it as the platform's own rule.

## Hard constraints (never override, never card as a question)

- B-roll ≤2.5s per shot, ≤20% of total runtime, never two consecutive b-roll shots back to back,
  no b-roll before 3.0s into the video.
- Open on the product itself (a real screen, a real result) — never a stock-footage or scene-setting
  cold open.
- Vocabulary and claims are per company, not universal: banned terms, numeric-claim restrictions,
  and any other wording rule: follow the company pack's `claims_policy` and that company's own
  video-producer dossier; where neither states them, DEGRADED — never invent, assume, or carry over another company's list
  (see `INDEX.md`'s precedence note). The list governs narration, on-screen text, and any copy built from the video. **Where
  the company's list restricts a claim type (for example numeric time or accuracy claims) and a
  customer says such a number on camera,** do not cut the video around it as if endorsing it —
  either omit the claim from the edit or, if it must stay for authenticity, do not amplify it in
  copy, titles, or ad text built from the clip.
- Viewing review: the rendered cut is watched — contact sheets against the timed transcript, per shot (primer §4/§7) — before any upload; the seat's own frame-viewing review satisfies this, no separate human viewer is required (uploaded or scheduled anywhere public).
- The cross-company rules above are operator rulings; the vocabulary/claims list is the company's
  own. Do not ask whether either still applies — read them, and apply them.

## 1. What each format is actually for

- **Testimonial** — a single person's short, credible endorsement. Optimized for social feed / paid
  ad placement. 30–90 seconds finished.
- **Case study** — a fuller story: who they are, what was broken, what changed, what it's like now.
  Optimized for a landing page, sales deck, or nurture email. 90 seconds–3 minutes finished.
- **Before/after** — a visual-contrast format nested inside either of the above: show the prior state
  next to the current state so the viewer sees the change rather than only hearing about it. Not a
  standalone format on its own — it's a technique applied within a testimonial or case study.
  (InLight Films; Concurate — before/after case study framing.)
- All three should read as one story: problem → what changed → what it's like now. Do not open with
  "tell us about the results" — open with who they are and what they do. (Storimatic Studio;
  Fourside Studios.)

## 2. Interview question sets

Ask questions in advance so the subject can think of real examples — never so they memorize a
script. Ask one question at a time; let them finish. (Fourside Studios; Say About Us.)

**Warm-up / background (always first, cut from final edit or used as a cold intro line):**
- Tell me about your business — what do you do, and for whom?
- What's your role, and how long have you been doing this work?

**The problem (before):**
- What was the situation like before you found us? Be specific — what wasn't working?
- What had you already tried? Why didn't it hold up?
- What finally made you go looking for something else?

**The decision:**
- What almost stopped you from trying this?
- What made you choose to move forward anyway?
- What were you nervous about, if anything?

**The experience (solution):**
- Walk me through what it was actually like to use it / work with us.
- What surprised you — good or bad?
- Was there a specific moment things clicked?

**The outcome (after) — phrase for verifiable answers that survive the company's claims list (see
§5):**
- What's different now, day to day?
- What can you do now that you couldn't before?
- If a friend in the same position asked you about this, what would you tell them?

**Close:**
- Anything you wish you'd known going in?
- Anything else you want people to know?

**Format-length guide** (Say About Us):
- Social clip (15–30s): one question, one strong answer.
- Standard testimonial (60–90s): 4–5 questions, one per story beat.
- Case study (2–3 min): up to 6 questions with room for one follow-up each.

**On-camera answer technique:** ask the subject to restate the question in their answer ("What I
like most is...") so the question can be cut out in editing without losing context. (Mighty Fine
Production.)

**Pre-call:** a short call days before the shoot — not to script them, but to surface which stories
and details are strongest, and to let them get comfortable on camera. (Fourside Studios.)

## 3. Consent and release — do not shoot without it

Get a signed release BEFORE recording, or immediately after and before any edit leaves the building.
A verbal "sure, go ahead" is not sufficient for commercial use. (Jotform; Rev.)

**A release should cover, at minimum** (Selene the Lawyer; Rev; 123FormBuilder):
1. Grant of rights to use their name, voice, image/video, and words in marketing — specify the
   channels (website, social, ads, email, sales decks, print) rather than leaving it unstated.
2. Whether the grant is perpetual or time-limited, and revocable or not — pick one; don't leave
   it ambiguous. A shorter, clearly time-boxed grant is easier to get signed and is fine to use if
   that's what's offered.
3. Right to edit for length, clarity, and grammar while preserving the substance of what they said.
4. No additional compensation is owed for use of the testimonial (state this plainly if true).
5. The subject confirms what they said is true and their own words/experience (originality/no
   plagiarism from someone else's review).
6. A liability/privacy waiver covering association with the business publicly.
7. If the subject is a minor, a parent/guardian must also sign.
8. Company name, subject's name/title, date, and a signature (wet or electronic) — keep the signed
   copy on file, tied to the specific video/photo it covers.
- Prefer a plain-language, short release over a dense legal document — a subject who doesn't
  understand what they signed is a bigger risk than a slightly narrower grant.
- Keep releases indexed by subject and by which asset(s) they cover — retrieving "do we have
  permission for this clip" must be fast, not a search.

## 4. Structure — the five-beat arc

1. **Hook (first ~15s, and it must be product, not scene-setting — see hard constraints):** the
   single clearest before/after moment or the strongest line from the interview. Lead with this,
   not with company background.
2. **Problem (~20s):** what wasn't working, in the subject's own words.
3. **Solution / experience (~20s):** what changed, what it was like to adopt it, any friction that
   got resolved.
4. **Outcome (~20–25s):** what's true now that wasn't before — described qualitatively per §5, shown
   visually wherever possible (see §6).
5. **Recommendation / close (~10–15s):** who this is for, would they recommend it, final thought.
(Say About Us five-act structure; Concurate problem/solution/results; InLight Films
before-during-after.)

- Front-load the transformation regardless of total runtime — don't make the viewer wait for the
  payoff. (mypromovideos.)
- Support every spoken claim with something visual on screen wherever one exists: a real screen
  recording, a real screenshot, a document, a photo — not just a talking head over b-roll.
  (Serpwatch; Zight.)
- Side-by-side or split-screen before/after shots are the strongest visual contrast device when
  footage supports it — use sparingly and keep within the b-roll budget in the hard constraints.

## 5. Avoiding unverifiable claims (where a company's claims list and normal marketing advice
diverge — follow the company's list)

Standard testimonial-marketing practice pushes hard toward specific numbers ("saved 15 hours a
week," "went from 72% to 91%") because vague praise reads as filler and is less persuasive
(Mailchimp; general practitioner consensus). **Where the company's claims list forbids a claim type
(for example numeric time or accuracy figures), that list overrides this general practice** —
regardless of what a customer says on camera or what general best practice recommends. Apply the
general "be specific, not vague" principle everywhere else (name the actual problem, the actual
task, the actual before/after state) without leaning on any claim type the company's list forbids.

**FTC baseline, for any number that IS used (non-time, non-accuracy) in a testimonial** — 16 CFR
Part 255, updated 2023, current as of this writing (FTC; eCFR; Davis Wright Tremaine):
- A testimonial depicting a specific result must reflect what buyers can generally expect, OR the ad
  must clearly and conspicuously disclose the results a typical buyer should expect. A "results not
  typical" disclaimer in small print is explicitly insufficient.
- Never publish a testimonial claim you cannot substantiate with real evidence — the claim itself
  becomes deceptive regardless of any disclosure, if it's simply untrue or unproven.
- If in doubt whether a specific number from a customer is typical or substantiated, cut it from the
  edit rather than publish and hope; a qualitative version of the same statement ("it's noticeably
  faster now" rather than a number) is the safer, still-authentic default.
- Never write a testimonial line for a customer to read verbatim if it contains a number or claim
  they didn't originate and can't personally stand behind.

**Practical filters before any claim ships, numeric or not:**
- Can we point to where this came from (their own words, a document, a real screen)? If not, cut it.
- Is this the customer's own account of their own experience, or our inference about what "probably"
  happened to them? Only the former belongs in their testimonial.
- Would this line survive the customer being asked, on the record, "is that still true"?

## 6. Using real screenshots and screen recordings as proof

- A real screen recording of the actual product in actual use is stronger evidence than a narrated
  claim or a static screenshot — it lets the viewer watch the thing happen rather than take someone's
  word for it. (Serpwatch; Zight.)
- Screenshots describe; screen recordings prove a workflow — prefer recordings when the point is "and
  then this happened," reserve static screenshots for a single before/after visual comparison.
- Always capture screen proof from the real, live product — never a mockup, a designed comp, or a
  staged/fake UI standing in for the real thing. A viewer who later notices the UI doesn't match the
  real product destroys the credibility the proof was meant to build.
- Get the customer's screen, not a generic demo account, whenever the story is about THEIR result —
  a generic account undercuts the specific-to-this-customer claim the case study is making.
- Pair every screen-proof clip with a plain caption stating what's on screen, since these clips are
  frequently watched muted on social feeds.
- Redact or blur anything in a screen recording that the subject didn't explicitly agree to expose
  (other customers' data, unrelated financials, other people's names) — get sign-off on the specific
  clip, not just a blanket release, when a recording shows their live account.

## 7. Shoot mechanics (remote or in-person)

- **Audio is the priority over video quality** — a clear-audio, lower-resolution clip beats sharp
  video with muddy or noisy sound. Use an external mic or a dedicated headset over a laptop's
  built-in mic whenever possible. (1to1 Studios; Riverside.)
- **Lighting**: put light in front of the subject's face, not behind them (no window backlight, no
  silhouette). Avoid straight-overhead light, which shadows the eyes/nose. Natural window light
  facing the subject, or a simple front-facing light, is enough. (Interview Guys; Digital
  Storyteller.)
- **Camera at eye level**, not looking up or down at the subject.
- **Keep rolling** between questions rather than stopping and starting — subjects relax and give
  better answers when they're not watching for a "we're recording now" cue. (Mighty Fine Production.)
- For remote recording, use a tool that saves each participant's local audio/video as its own file
  rather than a single compressed call recording, so editing has full-quality source material.
  (1to1 Studios.)
- Always record a short, separate "room tone" / silence clip on location — useful for cleaning
  audio cuts later.

## 8. Editing and pre-publish checklist

- Cut the interviewer's questions out; keep the subject's restated-question answers so context
  survives on its own (see §2 technique).
- Enforce the b-roll hard limits during the edit, not as an afterthought — count b-roll seconds and
  shot placement against the ≤2.5s/shot, ≤20% runtime, no-back-to-back, none-before-3.0s rule before
  calling a cut final.
- Confirm the open is product-first per the hard constraint — literally check what is on screen in
  the first 3 seconds.
- Run every line of narration and on-screen text against the company's vocabulary/claims list
  (hard constraints above).
- Confirm every visible screenshot/recording is genuine product, not a mockup, and that anything
  sensitive is redacted per §6.
- Confirm a signed release is on file for every identifiable person and every piece of their
  business shown.
- The complete finished cut, full runtime, is watched per shot against the timed transcript
  (primer §4/§7; the seat's own frame-viewing review satisfies this) before it is scheduled or
  uploaded anywhere public — this is a hard constraint, not a suggestion; do not skip it because the edit
  "looks done."
