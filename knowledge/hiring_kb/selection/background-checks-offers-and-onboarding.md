# Background checks, offers, and onboarding

**What this file answers:** how to run an FCRA-compliant background check (standalone disclosure,
written authorization, pre-adverse and adverse action notices), ban-the-box / fair-chance laws and
who they cover, when a driving-record check is required for a driver role, drug-testing basics and
where state law narrows what an employer can do, license verification for regulated trades and
healthcare roles, what an offer letter should and should not say, Form I-9 timing and E-Verify,
W-4/state withholding, new-hire reporting to the state, where worker classification (contractor vs.
employee) is decided, workers' comp and required posters, and a first-week onboarding checklist.

This file is information, not legal, tax, or accounting advice. An employment attorney, a CPA, or
the state agency itself decides how a specific background-check program, offer letter, or
classification call should be built or defended for a specific company and state. Where state law
differs, Colorado, California, Washington, New York are called out by name; every other state
varies and must be checked before a multi-state process is finalized.

## 1. FCRA background checks — the federal floor for using a third-party consumer report

The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) governs any background check on a candidate performed by a
third-party "consumer reporting agency" (a background-check vendor) — not a check the employer runs
entirely itself from public records with no vendor involved, though the ban-the-box and
individualized-assessment rules in §2 still apply either way.

**[KB-HSEL-016]** Before ordering a background check, the employer must provide the candidate a
**clear and conspicuous, standalone written disclosure** that a consumer report may be obtained for
employment purposes — this disclosure cannot be buried inside a job application or combined with
other content — and must get the candidate's **written authorization** to run the check. The
employer must also certify to the background-check company that it provided the disclosure, got
authorization, will comply with FCRA, and will not use the information to discriminate unlawfully
(official, FTC, "Using Consumer Reports: What Employers Need to Know," retrieved 2026-09-28).

**[KB-HSEL-017]** Before taking **any adverse employment action** based even partly on the report
(not hiring, withdrawing an offer, not promoting), the employer must send the candidate a
**pre-adverse action notice** containing: (a) a copy of the actual consumer report relied on, and
(b) a copy of the CFPB's "Summary of Your Rights Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act" (official, FTC
"Using Consumer Reports," retrieved 2026-09-28, and 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)(A), corroborated via
third-party legal-compliance summaries, retrieved 2026-09-28). This step exists so the candidate can
actually see what the employer saw and correct an error before the decision is final — sending the
report itself, not just a description of it, is the point.

**[KB-HSEL-018]** FCRA does **not** set one fixed federal waiting period between the pre-adverse
notice and the final adverse action — the statute requires only that the notice happen before the
adverse action is taken, with a reasonable amount of time in between for the candidate to actually
review and respond. A commonly used practical waiting period is **around five business days**, but
that specific number is an industry practice, not a statutory requirement — treat any specific
number of days quoted for this waiting period as `UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation` against
the FCRA text itself, and default to whatever is genuinely long enough for a real response, longer
rather than shorter when in doubt (third-party, compliance-vendor secondary summaries of 15 U.S.C. §
1681b(b)(3), retrieved 2026-09-28; a narrow statutory exception exists for certain
Secretary-of-Transportation-regulated positions allowing notice within three business days after
the action in specific circumstances — not applicable to a typical small-business hire).

**[KB-HSEL-019]** After the waiting period, if the employer proceeds with the adverse action, it
must send a final **adverse action notice** (oral, written, or electronic) stating: the name,
address, and phone number of the consumer reporting agency that supplied the report; a statement
that the reporting agency did not make the hiring decision and cannot explain the reason for it; a
notice of the candidate's right to dispute the accuracy of the report with the reporting agency; and
a notice of the right to a free copy of the report from that agency within 60 days (official, FTC
"Using Consumer Reports," retrieved 2026-09-28).

**[KB-HSEL-020]** The document that must accompany the pre-adverse notice is the CFPB's own "Summary
of Your Rights Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act," published under Regulation V (12 CFR Part 1022,
Appendix K) — confirmed as an existing CFPB regulatory appendix by that title
(`UNVERIFIED (primary source unreachable: the appendix's full text returned only a table-of-contents
view at retrieval time, not the substantive text)` as to its exact current wording — use the CFPB's
or FTC's own current published version of this document when sending it, never a self-drafted
substitute, since the form itself is what the law requires be delivered) (official, CFPB,
Regulation V Appendix K listing, retrieved 2026-09-28).

**Decision rules:**
- Never run a background check without both the standalone disclosure and signed authorization on
  file first.
- Never take an adverse action based on a report without first sending the pre-adverse notice, the
  actual report, and the CFPB Summary of Rights, and waiting a genuinely reasonable period.
- Securely dispose of background-check reports and any information derived from them once they are
  no longer needed, using a method (shredding, secure deletion) that prevents reconstruction
  (official, FTC "Using Consumer Reports," retrieved 2026-09-28).
- Remember §3 of the companion file (`screening-scoring-and-interviews.md`): an incomplete or
  inconclusive background check is never treated as a failure — it holds the candidate at "needs
  review" until it resolves.

## 2. Ban-the-box / fair-chance laws — when criminal history can be asked

These laws restrict **when** in the hiring process an employer may ask about or act on criminal
history — they do not ban considering it altogether, but they require deferring the question and,
usually, doing an individualized, job-related assessment before using a conviction to deny
employment.

**[KB-HSEL-021]** Federal contractors and federal executive-branch agencies are covered by the
federal Fair Chance Act, which restricts federal agencies and contractors from asking about an
applicant's criminal history before a conditional offer of employment is made, for positions
connected to a federal contract (`UNVERIFIED (primary source unreachable: no specific,
directly-fetched U.S. DOL or OPM page describing the federal Fair Chance Act's contractor-specific
scope and effective date was retrieved during this pass — the substance above reflects this KB's own
prior general knowledge of the Act, not a source read this pass)` — verify the Act's current
contractor-specific scope, effective date, and mechanics directly against dol.gov or opm.gov before
relying on this for a company that holds or is bidding on a federal contract).

**[KB-HSEL-022]** California's Fair Chance Act (effective January 1, 2018, as later strengthened)
prohibits employers with **five or more employees** from asking about, or considering, an
applicant's conviction history **until after a conditional offer of employment has been made**. It
also bars publicizing that a position is closed to applicants with a criminal history. When a
post-offer conviction history check does turn something up, the employer must conduct an
**individualized assessment** — weighing the nature and seriousness of the offense, the time that
has passed, and the nature of the specific job — before deciding to withdraw the offer, and must
give the candidate a chance to respond before the decision is final (official — California Civil
Rights Department materials, retrieved 2026-09-28, corroborated by NELP's independent summary,
retrieved 2026-09-28; `UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation` as to the exact current
notice-and-response day counts specifically, which have been updated by regulation since the law's
original passage and were not independently re-derived from the current regulation text this pass —
re-verify those specific timelines against the Civil Rights Department's current regulations before
advising a California employer on exact timing).

**[KB-HSEL-023]** Colorado's Chance to Compete Act (effective for employers with 11+ employees
starting September 1, 2019, and for all employers as of September 1, 2021) prohibits employers from
asking about criminal history on an **initial written or electronic job application**, and from
stating in an application or job ad that a person with a criminal history may not apply, that a
background check is required, or that the employer will not consider applicants with a felony or
other criminal record — any of those statements in a posting is itself a violation, independent of
when in the process the employer actually asks the question. It does not
prohibit obtaining an applicant's **publicly available** criminal history at any time, and it
preserves exceptions where a specific criminal background disqualifies someone from a position by
law. On a violation, the state issues a notice to comply within **30 business days**; if the
employer does not comply, or complies and then commits another violation, CDLE may order a civil penalty
of **up to $1,000 for a second violation, or up to $2,500 for a later violation** (official — CDLE
INFO #9C, "Chance to Compete Act,"
`https://cdle.colorado.gov/sites/cdle/files/info_%239c_chance_to_compete_act_4.8.2026.pdf`, retrieved
2026-09-28 — the earlier "$25,000 on a subsequent violation" figure in a prior version of this claim
was wrong by 10x and is corrected here after reading CDLE's own document directly).

**[KB-HSEL-024]** Washington and New York, like many other states and a large number of cities, also
have their own ban-the-box or fair-chance laws with their own specific timing and individualized-
assessment requirements that differ in detail from California's and Colorado's — this file does not
have those states' specific mechanics independently verified during this pass;
`UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation` as a placeholder that a state-specific check is required
before running a hiring process that touches those states, rather than assuming California's or
Colorado's rules transfer directly.

**Decision rule:** default to **no criminal-history question of any kind — on the application, in an
interview, or in a background check — before a conditional offer of employment has been made.**
California's own rule (claim 022 above) already reaches past the application form to bar the
inquiry "until after a conditional offer," which includes interviews; treating "just don't ask on
the application" as sufficient would violate California's law on its own terms. **Job postings
themselves are also restricted**, independent of the application-form question: never write "must
pass a background check," "no felonies," or any similar screening statement in a posting — Colorado
(claim 023) treats a statement like this in a posting as its own violation, separate from asking the
question at the wrong stage. Where a posting must say anything about a background check at all, the
safe, generic language is: **"A background check may be conducted after a conditional offer."** If a
post-offer criminal-history check surfaces something, do a genuine individualized assessment (nature
of offense, time elapsed, actual relevance to the specific job) before acting on it, document that
assessment, and give the candidate a chance to respond before finalizing — this is the safer default
even in a state without a specific individualized-assessment statute.

## 3. Driving records for driver roles

**[KB-HSEL-025]** **FMCSA's driver-qualification rules are NOT limited to CDL holders.** 49 CFR §
390.5 defines a "commercial motor vehicle" (CMV) by weight and use, not by license class: any
self-propelled or towed vehicle used on a highway in interstate commerce with a gross vehicle weight
rating (GVWR) or gross combination weight rating (GCWR) — or actual gross weight — of **10,001
pounds or more** is a CMV, whether or not the driver holds a commercial driver's license. A
remodeling company's own truck towing a loaded dump trailer, or a one-ton pickup pulling equipment,
can easily cross that threshold in interstate operation even though no CDL is required to drive it
(a CDL generally attaches at a higher, 26,001+ lb combination threshold — UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation; not in
§390.5) (official, 49 CFR § 390.5 for the 10,001 lb definition,
retrieved 2026-09-28). For a driver of a covered CMV, 49 CFR § 391.23(a)(1) requires the motor
carrier to inquire, **within 30 days of the date the driver's employment begins**, to every state
driver's-licensing authority where the driver held a license or permit in the preceding 3 years, to
obtain that driver's 3-year Motor Vehicle Record (MVR); § 391.23(b) requires the MVR itself to be
placed in the driver qualification file within that same 30-day window. In addition, §391.25 requires an MVR inquiry and review at least once every 12 months for each
driver (official, 49 CFR §§ 391.23 and 391.25, retrieved
2026-09-28 — this corrects a prior version of this claim, which wrongly stated the inquiry window as
`UNVERIFIED` and separately implied FMCSA rules apply only to CDL-holding drivers).

**Decision rule:** do not assume "no CDL required" means FMCSA doesn't apply. Check the actual
vehicle's GVWR/GCWR and whether the role's driving is interstate — a company truck-and-trailer
combination at or above 10,001 lbs used in interstate commerce triggers FMCSA Part 391, including
the 30-day MVR inquiry, regardless of CDL status. Intrastate-only operation is governed by state
law, which varies — confirm with an attorney or FMCSA directly before assuming a non-CDL,
intrastate-only role is exempt. Any role that requires driving a company vehicle or driving as a core
job duty should have a driving-record check before the offer is finalized even where FMCSA does not
apply, as a reasonable, job-related screening step — treat it as part of the role's rubric (see the
companion file's §1) if driving is a genuine job requirement.

## 4. Drug testing basics — and where state law narrows what's allowed

**[KB-HSEL-026]** For positions genuinely covered by U.S. DOT safety-sensitive rules (49 CFR Part 40
— e.g., commercial drivers, pilots, and other federally regulated safety-sensitive transportation
roles), pre-employment, random, reasonable-suspicion, **post-accident, and return-to-duty and
follow-up** drug testing is federally **required**, testing for a standard drug panel and with a
parallel alcohol-testing requirement (`UNVERIFIED (primary source unreachable: 49 CFR Part 40 itself
was not independently read this pass — the substance above, including the specific drug panel and
whether pre-employment alcohol testing is required, reflects this KB's own prior general knowledge
of DOT testing categories, corroborated only by third-party SAMHSA/DOT-compliance summaries, not a
directly-fetched regulation text)` — a secondary source consulted this pass reported that
**pre-employment alcohol testing specifically is not required for FMCSA-covered drivers**, only
pre-employment drug testing; verify the exact panel, and which test types apply at which trigger
event, directly against 49 CFR Part 40 before building a compliance program around it).

**[KB-HSEL-027] Flag before building any drug-testing policy — state law varies widely and
sometimes conflicts with federal policy.** Outside DOT-covered safety-sensitive roles, whether and
how an employer may drug test (pre-employment, random, or otherwise), what substances may be tested
for, and how a positive test may be used, is governed heavily by **state law**, and several states
impose specific restrictions — for example, some states limit or prohibit testing for marijuana use
that would otherwise be lawful under that state's own cannabis law, require advance notice or a
specific testing protocol, or restrict random testing to safety-sensitive roles only.
`UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation` as to the specific current rules for Colorado, California,
Washington, and New York individually — this file does not have those confirmed during this pass.
**Decision rule:** treat drug testing as a policy area that needs a state-specific legal check
before implementation, not something to copy from a generic template — this is exactly the kind of
call that gets escalated to an employment attorney, never decided by the hiring agent alone.

## 5. License verification for licensed trades and healthcare roles

**[KB-HSEL-037]** (internal doctrine, retrieved 2026-09-28 — license-verification discipline
generalized from the clearance-vs-fit separation already sourced in the companion file's §§2-3; the
specific claim that a required license, permit, or certification must exist for a given trade or
healthcare role in a given state/locality is itself a state-specific fact this KB does not enumerate
— confirm the specific licensing requirement with the issuing state board before relying on it):

- For any role where state or local law requires a license, permit, or certification to legally
  perform the work (a licensed trade — electrical, plumbing, HVAC — or a healthcare role requiring
  state licensure or a national registry check), verify the license **directly against the issuing
  state board or registry**, not from a copy the candidate provides, before the person performs any
  work requiring that license.
- Treat license status as a **compliance-clearance** check (see the companion file's §2), never a
  fit-ranking input — a candidate with a temporarily lapsed but otherwise-qualified license is
  "needs review," not "reject," until the actual license status is confirmed one way or the other
  (companion file §3's "failed lookup never moves rank" rule applies identically here).
- Re-verify license status periodically for roles that require an active license to keep working
  (not just at hire) — an employer that never re-checks is exposed if a license lapses,
  is suspended, or is revoked after hire and the person keeps working in a role that requires it.
- For roles with a national exclusion/sanctions-list dimension (e.g., healthcare roles subject to
  federal program exclusion checks), that check follows the same clearance-not-rank logic: a
  confirmed match is disqualifying; an unreachable database is "needs review," never an automatic
  pass or fail.

## 6. Offer letters — at-will language, and what not to promise

**[KB-HSEL-028]** Courts have found that specific language in an offer letter — phrases like
"permanent position," "career opportunity," "we look forward to a long relationship," "job
security," or describing pay as an "annual salary" when only a current rate is meant — can be used
as evidence of an **implied contract** that limits the employer's ability to terminate at will, even
when the company did not intend to create one. The safer practice, corroborated across multiple
employment-law summaries, is to include clear at-will language near the end of the offer letter
(e.g., stating plainly that employment is at-will and may be ended by either party at any time, for
any lawful reason, with or without cause or notice) and to avoid language implying permanence,
guaranteed future raises or bonuses, or an indefinite relationship (third-party, corroborated across
multiple employment-law-practice summaries, retrieved 2026-09-28; `UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs
derivation` as to the exact SHRM-recommended wording quoted in some of these sources — treat any
specific suggested sentence as a starting point for attorney review, not a ready-to-use legal
clause). **Montana is a known exception to at-will employment generally** — its Wrongful Discharge
from Employment Act limits termination to good cause after a probationary period, unlike the
at-will default in every other state — `UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation`, as this KB has not
independently verified Montana's statute text or its exact probationary-period mechanics this pass.
**Decision rule for Montana specifically:** never draft a standard at-will offer letter for a
Montana-based hire without an attorney confirming how Montana's law changes the at-will language and
what, if anything, replaces it.

**Decision rules for an offer letter:**
- State the position title, reporting structure, start date, and compensation using the
  comp-wording discipline described elsewhere in this KB (lead with the guaranteed floor, never
  "or") — never promise a bonus, raise, or commission structure that hasn't actually been approved.
- Include clear at-will language, unless the operator has a real, attorney-reviewed employment
  contract in place for this role (at-will is the default absence of a contract, not the same
  thing as a contract that happens to be short).
- Avoid "permanent," "career," "long-term," "job security," "family," or similar language.
- Make any offer explicitly contingent on whatever it is actually contingent on (background check,
  license verification, I-9 verification, drug test where applicable) — state the contingency
  plainly rather than silently assuming the candidate understands the offer isn't final.
- Never let the agent (or anyone other than the operator) send a final signed offer — this is one of
  the non-negotiable approval gates that also apply to every other outbound candidate communication.

## 7. Form I-9 — timing

**[KB-HSEL-029]** Every U.S. employer must complete and retain Form I-9 for every person hired for
employment in the United States. **Section 1** (the employee's own information and attestation) must
be completed and signed by the employee **no later than their first day of employment** — it may be
completed any time after the employee accepts the job offer, but not before (official, USCIS,
"Completing Section 1, Employee Information and Attestation," retrieved 2026-09-28).

**[KB-HSEL-030]** **Section 2** (the employer's review and verification of the employee's
identity/work-authorization documents) must be completed **within three business days** of the
employee's date of hire (first day of work for pay) — for example, an employee starting on a Monday
must have Section 2 completed by Thursday of that same week. If the person is hired for less than
three business days total, Section 2 must be completed on the **first day** of employment (official
— USCIS, "Completing Section 2, Employer Review and Verification," read directly at uscis.gov,
retrieved 2026-09-28, and 8 CFR § 274a.2(b)(1)(ii)-(iii), retrieved 2026-09-28 — this corrects a
prior version of this claim, which tagged the three-business-day rule as third-party/M-274-sourced;
it is confirmed directly in both USCIS's own guidance and the regulation itself). The specific
dollar-amount per-form penalty figures and any characterization of a specific 2026 enforcement
policy circulating in secondary sources were **not** independently verified against a primary
government source during this pass and remain excluded here — `UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs
derivation` as to any specific penalty dollar figures; do not quote a specific fine amount to an
operator without checking USCIS's current penalty schedule directly.

**Decision rule:** treat the three-business-day Section 2 deadline as a hard operational deadline —
schedule the document-verification appointment (whether in person or via an authorized remote
process) as part of the offer-acceptance workflow, not as an afterthought for the new hire's first
week.

## 8. E-Verify — voluntary vs. required

**[KB-HSEL-031]** E-Verify is **voluntary** for most private employers in the United States, but is
**mandatory** for certain categories: federal contractors and subcontractors covered by the Federal
Acquisition Regulation (FAR) E-Verify clause, and employers in a number of states that have enacted
their own state-law E-Verify mandates (which vary by state — some require it for all employers,
some only for public employers or state contractors) (official, E-Verify.gov's own materials
describing the Federal Contractor Rule, retrieved 2026-09-28; the specific list of which states
currently mandate E-Verify, and for which employer categories, was not independently confirmed
during this pass — `UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation` as to the current state-by-state mandate
list; check the operator's own state's current requirement before assuming E-Verify is optional).

**Decision rule:** confirm whether the operator's state, or a specific contract the company holds,
makes E-Verify mandatory before treating it as optional; where it is genuinely optional, E-Verify
remains a reasonable additional step to confirm work authorization but does not replace Form I-9,
which is required regardless.

## 9. W-4 and state withholding

**[KB-HSEL-038]** (internal doctrine, retrieved 2026-09-28 — payroll/withholding-mechanics routing
rule; the specific fact that most income-tax states require their own withholding form is
`UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation`, not independently verified state-by-state this pass):

- Every new employee completes federal Form W-4 (or the employer's own approved digital equivalent)
  before or on their first day, so payroll can withhold federal income tax correctly.
- Most states with an income tax also require a state-specific withholding form (some states accept
  the federal W-4, many require their own version) — confirm the specific state's current form
  before onboarding a new hire there, especially for a company hiring across multiple states.
- This is a payroll/tax mechanics question, not a hiring-agent judgment call — route it to whatever
  payroll system or accountant the company already uses; the hiring agent's role is to make sure the
  form gets collected on time, not to advise on withholding elections.

## 10. New-hire reporting to the state

**[KB-HSEL-032]** Federal law (the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act
of 1996, codified at 42 U.S.C. § 653a) requires every employer to report seven required data
elements on every new and rehired employee — employee name, employee address, employee Social
Security number, **date of hire** (the date the employee first performs services for pay), employer
name, employer address, and the employer's Federal Employer Identification Number (FEIN) — to the
state's New Hire Reporting directory **within 20 days of the hire date**, feeding into the National
Directory of New Hires (used primarily for child-support enforcement); some states set a shorter
deadline than the federal 20-day maximum, and the employer must follow whichever deadline the
state where the employee works actually requires (official, 42 U.S.C. § 653a and Administration for
Children and Families (ACF), "New Hire Reporting," retrieved 2026-09-28 — this corrects a prior
version of this claim, which omitted "date of hire" from the required data-element list). **A
multistate employer** (one with employees working in more than one state) may choose either to
report each new hire to the state where that employee actually works, or to register with HHS as a
multistate employer, designate one single state to receive all its new-hire reports, and report
every new hire nationwide to that one chosen state instead (official, same ACF source, retrieved
2026-09-28).

**Decision rule:** report every new hire to the correct state's new-hire directory within the
state's own deadline (never assume 20 days is safe everywhere — some states require faster
reporting), collect all seven required data elements including date of hire, and treat this as a
standing first-week onboarding task, not an optional step. A company hiring in more than one state
should decide once, deliberately, whether to report per-state or register for the single-state
multistate-employer option — not default to per-state reporting without considering the
administrative trade-off.

## 11. Worker classification (contractor vs. employee)

Classifying a worker as an independent contractor instead of an employee to save on payroll tax or
benefits, without an actual classification analysis, is one of the highest-risk shortcuts in hiring
— it exposes the company to back taxes, back wages, and penalties if successfully challenged. This
file does not repeat the classification tests here: see
[`~/.claude/knowledge/venture_kb/recruiting/equity-and-agreements.md`](../../venture_kb/recruiting/equity-and-agreements.md)
§9, which covers the IRS common-law test, the evolving federal DOL/FLSA classification standard, and
state-level ABC tests, with its own sourced claims (`KB-VREC-1xx`). **Decision rule:** never let a
classification decision be made purely to save money — route it through the actual test analysis in
that file, and involve an employment attorney or CPA before classifying any ongoing role as a
contractor rather than an employee.

## 12. Workers' compensation and required posters

**[KB-HSEL-039]** **Workers' compensation insurance** is required in nearly every state for an
employer with employees (thresholds and exact rules vary — some states exempt very small employers
or specific industries) — confirm the specific requirement for the operator's state before the first
employee starts work; this is a state-insurance-regulator question to flag, not something to assume
is optional. `UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation` as to the exact threshold in any specific state
— flag for the operator/an insurance broker rather than asserting a specific number.
- **[KB-HSEL-033]** Federal OSHA requires most private-sector employers with one or more employees
  to display the "Job Safety and Health: It's the Law" poster (OSHA Publication 3165) in a
  conspicuous location accessible to all employees (official, OSHA, "OSHA Poster," retrieved
  2026-09-28). Additional federal posters (EEO, FMLA where applicable, minimum wage/FLSA, and
  others) and state-specific required posters also apply — the exact combination depends on
  employer size and state; `UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation` as to the complete current list
  for any specific state — flag for the operator to confirm via their state labor department or a
  poster-compliance service, since posting requirements change periodically and an outdated poster
  is itself a compliance gap.

## 13. First-week onboarding checklist

**[KB-HSEL-040]** (internal doctrine, retrieved 2026-09-28 — a sequencing checklist assembled from
this file's own already-sourced deadlines (§7 I-9, §10 new-hire reporting) and this KB's general
practice discipline; not itself a new legal source beyond what those sections already cite):

A practical, sequenced checklist for a new hire's first week — treat as a template to adapt per
company, not a rigid script:

**Before day one:**
- [ ] Signed offer letter on file, with all contingencies (background check, license verification,
  I-9) resolved or explicitly still pending with a clear owner.
- [ ] Start date, reporting manager, and schedule confirmed with the new hire in writing.
- [ ] Payroll/HRIS record created; W-4 and any required state withholding form sent for completion.
- [ ] Equipment, system access, and any required credentials/accounts requested so they're ready
  day one (never provisioned before the background check/clearance step in §1–§5 has actually
  cleared, for any role where that check gates the hire).

**Day one:**
- [ ] Form I-9 Section 1 completed by the employee (§7).
- [ ] Section 2 document review scheduled or completed (must land within 3 business days — §7).
- [ ] New-hire orientation: company policies, safety training, required posters/notices pointed out
  (§12), reporting structure, first assignments.
- [ ] Confirm the new hire knows who to go to with questions in week one.

**Within the first week:**
- [ ] Form I-9 Section 2 completed (no later than 3 business days after start — §7).
- [ ] New-hire report filed with the state (within the state's own deadline, never later than the
  federal 20-day maximum — §10).
- [ ] Any role-specific license/certification re-confirmed as active if there was any gap between
  the offer and the start date.
- [ ] First check-in scheduled (informal 1:1, not a formal review) to catch early friction while
  it's still cheap to fix.

## Decision rules (summary)

1. Never run a third-party background check without the standalone disclosure and signed
   authorization on file first.
2. Never take an adverse action from a background check without the pre-adverse notice (report +
   CFPB Summary of Rights), a genuinely reasonable wait, and then the final adverse-action notice.
3. Default to never asking about criminal history on the initial application, anywhere — defer to
   post-offer, with a real individualized assessment before acting on anything it surfaces.
4. Any role with driving as a real job duty gets a driving-record check before the offer is
   finalized; CDL/FMCSA-covered roles get an annual re-check too.
5. Route any drug-testing policy decision outside DOT-mandated safety-sensitive roles through
   state-specific legal review before implementing it.
6. Verify a required license directly against the issuing authority, treat status as clearance (not
   fit-rank), and re-verify periodically for roles requiring an active license to keep working.
7. Offer letters state at-will terms plainly and avoid "permanent," "career," "job security," or
   similar language that courts have read as implying a contract.
8. Complete I-9 Section 1 by day one and Section 2 within 3 business days — no exceptions.
9. Report every new hire to the correct state new-hire directory within that state's deadline.
10. Never classify a role as contractor-vs-employee to save money without running the actual
    classification test in the linked venture_kb file.

## Anti-patterns

- Embedding the background-check disclosure inside the job application instead of as a standalone
  document.
- Taking an adverse action the same day the background report comes back, with no pre-adverse notice
  or waiting period.
- Asking about criminal history on the initial application "just to save a step."
- Treating a lapsed-but-in-process license renewal as an automatic reject instead of "needs review."
- Writing an offer letter that promises "a long career here" or an unapproved future bonus structure
  because it sounded encouraging.
- Missing the I-9 Section 2 three-business-day deadline because onboarding paperwork wasn't
  scheduled until the new hire's first full week.
- Classifying a full-time, ongoing role as a 1099 contractor purely because it's cheaper, without
  ever running the classification test.
- Assuming workers' comp or state posting requirements don't apply because the company is small,
  without actually checking the state's specific threshold.
