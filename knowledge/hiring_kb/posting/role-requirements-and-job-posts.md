# Role Requirements and Job Posts

## What this file answers

How to turn a hiring need into a written requirements list (must-have vs. nice-to-have,
job-related and legally defensible), how to structure and write the actual job post (title,
summary, duties, requirements, pay, benefits, schedule, location, how to apply), how to keep the
language EEOC/discrimination-safe, how to word essential functions under the ADA, how to write pay
figures so they read as confident rather than unstable, and which US states currently require a
pay range and benefits description in every posting. This file is upstream of
`job-boards-and-indeed.md`, which covers where the finished post gets distributed.

This file is company-agnostic. It applies equally to a remodeling company hiring a lead carpenter,
a small SaaS company hiring an account executive, and a dental office hiring a front-desk
coordinator — the mechanics below are the same; only the specific duties and credentials change
per role.

**Legal content is information, not advice.** Nothing here is a substitute for a licensed
employment attorney, and several items below (state coverage, exact pay-transparency mechanics,
whether a specific job qualifies for an exemption) should be confirmed with one before a company
relies on them for a high-stakes decision. Where this file says "an attorney should confirm," treat
that as a hard stop, not a suggestion.

## 1. Turning a hiring need into requirements

### 1.1 Must-have vs. nice-to-have — the only test that matters

**[KB-HPOST-001]** A requirement belongs on the must-have list only if it is genuinely necessary to
perform the job — **job-related and consistent with business necessity** — or a legally required
credential (a license, a certification, a clearance the work cannot be performed without).
Everything else, no matter how much the hiring manager wants it, is a nice-to-have. This is not the
same thing as a "bona fide occupational qualification (BFOQ)": a BFOQ is a narrow statutory defense
that lets an employer openly use sex, religion, national origin, or age as a hiring criterion in the
rare case the job genuinely requires it (never race) — see §3.4 — and it is not the test that
applies to a neutral requirement like a license or years of experience. The test that applies here
is the one Title VII and the EEOC use for a neutral selection criterion: it must be job-related and
consistent with business necessity, and it is a defense an employer must prove, not a default state
(official — 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(k); EEOC, "Employment Tests and Selection Procedures,"
`eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/employment-tests-and-selection-procedures`, retrieved 2026-09-28).

**[KB-HPOST-002]** Decision rule for sorting any candidate criterion:

- **Ask: "Can the job actually be done without this?"** If yes — even done less smoothly, even
  done by someone who'd need six weeks to ramp up — it is a nice-to-have, not a must-have.
- **Ask: "Is this requirement about the person's identity, background, or lifestyle rather than
  the work itself?"** (age, sex, religion, national origin, marital/family status, "culture fit,"
  a general physical stereotype not tied to a specific job task) — if so, it is never a legitimate
  requirement at all; see §3.
- **Ask: "Would we actually reject an otherwise-perfect candidate for lacking this?"** If the
  honest answer is no, it does not belong on the must-have list even if it is written there today.
  A must-have list padded with wishes is a discrimination-exposure and pay-transparency-accuracy
  problem at once — an inflated requirements list makes it harder to write an honest pay range
  (§5) and creates a paper trail that can be read as a pretext if a rejected candidate later
  challenges the decision.

**[KB-HPOST-003]** A generalizable pattern for defining a role's requirements, independent of
industry: separate the **credential/compliance layer** (things that are checked as pass/fail —
licensed, insured, background-check-clean, work-authorized) from the **fit/scoring layer**
(skills, experience, schedule match, pay-expectation match) — never blend the two into one list.
The credential layer gates whether someone can be hired at all; the fit layer only ranks how good a
match they are among people who already clear the gate (internal doctrine — this separation is
what keeps a slow or inconclusive background check, a missing license-verification response, or
any other infrastructure delay from being treated as a rejection signal; the same failure mode this
doctrine guards against recurs across very different professions: license lookups, degree
verification, reference-check no-shows, and immigration-status verification all have the same
"slow or inconclusive is not the same as failed" property). See
`../selection/screening-scoring-and-interviews.md` §§1-3 for the full pattern.

**[KB-HPOST-004]** Practical must-have categories that are almost always genuinely job-related and
necessary when the job actually requires them: a specific license or certification required by law
to perform the
work (a journeyman electrician's license, a state contractor's license, a CPA license, a nursing
or dental hygiene license); liability insurance or bondability where the role involves handling
money, driving a company vehicle, or working in a customer's home; a valid driver's license where
driving is an actual job duty (not merely convenient); specific years of hands-on experience with
a named tool, system, or code requirement where the work is genuinely too specialized to train on
the job in a reasonable window; language fluency where the job itself requires communicating in
that language with customers or reading code-required documentation (internal doctrine).

### 1.2 Interviewing the hiring need before writing anything

**[KB-HPOST-005]** Before drafting a post, the company should be able to answer, in plain
language: what will this person actually do in their first 90 days; what happens today because
this role is empty (the pain the hire solves); what is the realistic pay band the company can
actually pay (not aspirational — see §5); what schedule and location constraints are real
constraints vs. preferences; and what would make the company say no to an otherwise-strong
candidate. An agent drafting requirements on the company's behalf should ask these questions
rather than inventing an idealized candidate profile from a generic template (internal doctrine).

## 2. Writing the job post

### 2.1 Structure

**[KB-HPOST-006]** A complete job post has, at minimum, these sections, in roughly this order:
**title** (the actual role name a candidate would search for — not an internal or inflated title);
**summary** (2-4 sentences: what the company does, what this role does, why it exists);
**duties/responsibilities** (a bulleted list of what the person will actually do, framed around
outcomes and tasks, not personality traits); **requirements** (must-haves clearly separated from
nice-to-haves — label them, don't blend them); **pay** (see §5 for exact wording rules); **benefits**
(what's offered — health coverage, PTO, retirement match, tools/vehicle provided, training); **schedule**
(hours, shift pattern, on-call expectations, remote/hybrid/onsite); **location** (the actual work
site or service area — not a vague "remote-friendly" if the job requires physical presence);
**how to apply** (the exact channel and what to submit — resume, work samples, license number).
This shape mirrors what most state pay-transparency laws now require as a baseline and what
Indeed's own quality guidance rewards (see `job-boards-and-indeed.md` §2) (internal doctrine,
cross-referenced against the state requirements in §6 below).

**[KB-HPOST-007]** Write duties as verbs tied to outcomes ("install and finish drywall to a
Level 4 finish on residential remodel jobs," "respond to inbound support tickets within a 4-hour
SLA," "greet patients and manage the day's appointment schedule") rather than as adjectives about
the person ("must be a self-starter," "team player," "hungry"). Adjective-heavy duty lists are
both less useful to a candidate deciding whether to apply and more likely to read as a personality
screen than a job description — see §3 on "culture fit" language specifically (internal doctrine).

### 2.2 Title and summary

**[KB-HPOST-008]** Use the plain, searchable title a candidate would actually type into a job
board's search box, not an internal title inflated for ego or deflated for pay-grade reasons
("Growth Ninja" instead of "Marketing Coordinator," or "Junior" attached to a role that actually
requires five years of experience). A mismatched title both suppresses the post's visibility on
keyword-driven job boards and sets a false first impression that a mismatched requirements list
later corrects — costing the applicant's trust before the first message (internal doctrine, plus
see `job-boards-and-indeed.md` for how Indeed's own matching depends on this).

## 3. EEO/discrimination-safe wording

### 3.1 Who is covered and why it matters even for a very small company

**[KB-HPOST-009]** Federal coverage thresholds, as stated by the EEOC: employers with **15 or more
employees** are covered for discrimination claims based on race, color, religion, sex (including
pregnancy, sexual orientation, and transgender status), national origin, disability, and genetic
information; employers with **20 or more employees** are additionally covered for age
discrimination (40+) under the ADEA; the **Equal Pay Act covers virtually all employers**
regardless of size (official — EEOC, "Small Business Requirements,"
`eeoc.gov/employers/small-business/small-business-requirements`, retrieved 2026-09-28; and EEOC,
"Coverage of Business/Private Employers," `eeoc.gov/employers/coverage-businessprivate-employers`,
retrieved 2026-09-28).

**[KB-HPOST-010]** **Decision rule:** even a company below the federal 15-employee threshold should
still follow every rule in this section. Most states and many cities have their own
anti-discrimination laws with lower or no employee-count thresholds (a fact this file flags
generically — the exact state/local threshold is a state-specific lookup an attorney should
confirm for the company's actual location), and discriminatory language in a public job post
carries reputational and platform-policy risk (Indeed and other boards remove posts with
discriminatory language regardless of the company's legal coverage — see
`job-boards-and-indeed.md`) independent of whether a lawsuit could technically be filed
(internal doctrine).

### 3.2 What the EEOC actually prohibits in recruitment and advertising

**[KB-HPOST-011]** The EEOC's own guidance states plainly that a "help-wanted ad that seeks
'females' or 'recent college graduates'" can illegally discourage applicants on the basis of sex
or age even without saying "no men" or "no older workers" outright — a preference expressed
positively for one group is treated the same as an exclusion of another (official — EEOC,
"Prohibited Employment Policies/Practices," `eeoc.gov/prohibited-employment-policiespractices`,
retrieved 2026-09-28).

**[KB-HPOST-012]** The same guidance prohibits recruitment methods that exclude protected groups
even when no discriminatory intent is stated — the EEOC's own example is that "an employer's
reliance on word-of-mouth recruitment by its mostly Hispanic work force" can violate the law if it
produces disproportionate hiring patterns, because it functions as a barrier to applicants outside
that existing network (official — same source as claim 011, retrieved 2026-09-28). **Practical
takeaway:** a company that only ever hires through referrals from its current crew should
supplement with an open posting channel (see `job-boards-and-indeed.md`) rather than relying on
word-of-mouth alone, precisely to avoid this pattern.

**[KB-HPOST-013]** Protected characteristics a job post or recruitment process must never use to
discourage or prefer applicants, per EEOC guidance: race, color, religion, sex (including
pregnancy, sexual orientation, and transgender status), national origin, age (40+), disability, and
genetic information (official — same source as claim 011, retrieved 2026-09-28).

### 3.3 Specific wording traps

**[KB-HPOST-014]** **Age.** Never state or imply a preferred age range or generation, including
indirectly. Phrases like "digital native," "recent grad," "energetic," or an upper age cap for
non-safety-critical roles all function as age signals. An age requirement is lawful only where the
job genuinely requires it by law (e.g., minimum age to serve alcohol, minimum age for certain
equipment operation) — never as a generic culture preference (official — Indeed's own employer
discrimination policy states "jobs may not show preference or exclusion based on age," permits a
minimum-age requirement "only if required by labor laws or the nature of the job (for example,
serving alcohol)," and specifically names "digital native" as a disallowed generational-preference
phrase; `indeed.com/help/employers/articles/discrimination-and-hate-offenses`, retrieved 2026-09-28
— cited here as a platform-enforcement mirror of the same EEOC principle in claims 011-013, not as a
substitute for the underlying federal law).

**[KB-HPOST-015]** **Sex.** Use neutral job titles ("chairperson," "salesperson," "flight
attendant") rather than gendered ones ("chairman," "salesman," "stewardess"), and never state or
imply a preference for one gender, gender identity, or sexual orientation — including through
coded language about who would be a "good fit" for a physically demanding or customer-facing role
(official — same Indeed source as claim 014, retrieved 2026-09-28, echoing EEOC's sex-discrimination
coverage in claim 013).

**[KB-HPOST-016]** **National origin.** Never require a specific national origin, ethnicity, or
accent. A language requirement is lawful only when phrased around the skill itself and tied to an
actual job need — "fluent in Spanish and English" is acceptable language framing when the job
genuinely requires bilingual communication with customers; "English must be your first language"
is not, because it screens by origin rather than by actual competency (official — same Indeed
source as claim 014, retrieved 2026-09-28).

**[KB-HPOST-017]** **Disability.** A job post must never mention disability status, and must never
ask (directly or through a "requirements" list) whether a candidate has a medical condition. See
§4 for the full ADA essential-functions framework, which is the correct tool for describing
physical or cognitive job demands without asking about disability status.

**[KB-HPOST-018]** **"Culture fit."** Treat "culture fit," "team fit," or similar language as a
red flag to rewrite, not a legitimate requirement category, unless it can be translated into a
specific, job-related, observable behavior ("comfortable receiving direct feedback in a fast-paced
kitchen," "willing to work rotating weekend shifts"). Undefined "fit" language is exactly the kind
of criterion most likely to smuggle in an identity-based preference (age, appearance, background)
without naming it directly, and it gives no candidate a fair way to self-assess whether they should
apply (internal doctrine, directly generalizing the EEOC's own "help-wanted ad" example in claim
011 — an ad that filters by an unstated, non-job-related trait is treated the same as one that
states it outright).

**[KB-HPOST-019]** **Physical requirements** must be described as specific, measurable job tasks
("able to lift and carry 50 lbs of tile and mortar bags up a ladder repeatedly during a shift," "able
to stand for the duration of a 6-hour shift") rather than as a generic trait ("must be strong," "must
be in good physical condition," "no health issues"). This both keeps the posting ADA-compliant
(see §4) and gives a candidate with a disability a fair, specific basis to self-assess whether they
can perform the function with or without an accommodation (official — EEOC, "Job Applicants and
the ADA," `eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/job-applicants-and-ada`, retrieved 2026-09-28).

### 3.4 Decision rules — EEO wording

**A bona fide occupational qualification (BFOQ)** is a narrow statutory defense that lets an
employer openly use sex, religion, national origin, or age as a hiring criterion — **never race,
which has no BFOQ defense at all** — where that trait is reasonably necessary to the normal
operation of the particular business (e.g., a role requiring an actor of a specific sex for
authenticity). It is not the test used for a neutral requirement like a license, insurance, or years
of experience — those are judged as job-related and consistent with business necessity (§1.1,
`[KB-HPOST-001]`). BFOQ is rare, narrowly construed by courts, and an attorney decides before a
company relies on it (official, 42 U.S.C. §2000e-2(e); age BFOQ at 29 U.S.C. §623(f)(1), retrieved
2026-09-28).

- If a requirement describes the *work*, keep it (rewritten in specific, measurable terms if it
  isn't already).
- If a requirement describes the *person's identity or background*, delete it, unless it is a
  narrow, provable BFOQ (rare — confirm with an attorney before relying on this exception).
- If in doubt whether a phrase reads as coded language for a protected trait, rewrite it around an
  observable job behavior instead of guessing whether it's "probably fine."
- Never publish a post with a stated age, sex, national-origin, religion, or disability preference
  or exclusion, even phrased as an aspiration ("looking for someone young and energetic").

### 3.5 Anti-patterns

- Padding a requirements list with generic "soft skill" adjectives that function as a personality
  or age screen rather than describing the job.
- Writing "recent college graduate" or "digital native" to signal a youth preference without
  naming age directly.
- Relying only on referral/word-of-mouth recruiting for every open role.
- Treating "culture fit" as self-justifying instead of translating it into an observable, job-related
  behavior.
- Asking about physical ability in vague, medical-sounding terms instead of specific job tasks.

## 4. ADA essential-functions wording

**[KB-HPOST-020]** "Essential functions" are the fundamental duties of the job — the tasks so
central to the role that removing them would fundamentally change what the job is. The ADA
protects a qualified applicant's right to be considered if they can perform the essential functions
either on their own or with a reasonable accommodation; an employer cannot reject a candidate
solely for being unable to perform a *marginal* (non-essential) duty (official — EEOC, "Job
Applicants and the ADA," `eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/job-applicants-and-ada`, retrieved 2026-09-28).

**[KB-HPOST-021]** The ADA does not require an employer to write or maintain a job description at
all — but if one exists and was prepared *before* the job was advertised or candidates were
interviewed, it is treated as evidence (though not conclusive evidence) of what the job's essential
functions actually are (official — 42 U.S.C. § 12111(8), which lists a written job description
prepared before advertising or interviewing as evidence of essential functions — the EEOC's "Job
Applicants and the ADA" page cited elsewhere in this section does not itself contain this rule,
retrieved 2026-09-28). **Practical
takeaway: write the essential-functions list before posting the job, not after a specific
candidate's limitations come to light** — a functions list written reactively, after a disability
becomes known, looks exactly like a post-hoc justification for exclusion, which is the opposite of
its intended protective purpose.

**[KB-HPOST-022]** Avoid open-ended catch-all language like "other duties as assigned" when listing
essential functions specifically — a catch-all phrase is not itself evidence of what is essential or
marginal, since its vagueness undercuts its evidentiary value either way (internal doctrine — the
EEOC's "Job Applicants and the ADA" page does not contain this specific guidance; I could not locate
a primary EEOC source stating it during this pass, so this is stated here as this KB's own practical
rule, not an EEOC citation). If a catch-all is needed for operational flexibility, keep it in the
post but do not lean on it to defend a specific essential-function determination later.

**[KB-HPOST-023]** Pre-offer, an employer **may** ask an applicant whether they can perform a
specific job function (essential or marginal), including demonstrating it, but **may not** ask
disability-related questions — e.g., "do you have a disability that would interfere with your
ability to do this job," questions about medications, mental-health treatment, or workers'
compensation history. An employer may ask whether the candidate needs an accommodation for the
*hiring process itself* (an accessible interview location, an interpreter, extra time on a test),
but not whether they'll need one to perform the job once hired (official — same source as claim
020, retrieved 2026-09-28).

**[KB-HPOST-024]** After a **conditional offer** has been made, an employer may require a medical
exam and ask disability-related questions, and may only withdraw the offer if the individual cannot
perform the essential functions (with or without accommodation) or poses a direct threat of
substantial harm that cannot be reduced by accommodation — never based on generalized assumptions
or speculation about a condition (official — same source as claim 020, retrieved 2026-09-28).

**[KB-HPOST-025]** An employer must provide reasonable accommodation during the hiring process
itself (alternative test formats, accessible interview locations, sign-language interpreters)
unless doing so would cause "undue hardship" (significant difficulty or expense) — and cannot
refuse to consider a candidate simply because they requested an accommodation (official — same
source as claim 020, retrieved 2026-09-28).

### 4.1 Decision rules — ADA essential functions

- Write the essential-functions list at requirements time (§1), before any candidate is in view.
- Phrase every physical/cognitive demand as a specific, observable job task (see claim 019), never
  as a vague health trait.
- Before any conditional offer: no medical questions, no disability questions, no "can you handle
  the stress of X" questions that fish for a mental-health answer.
- After a conditional offer, medical inquiry is allowed but must be tied to the ability to perform
  essential functions or a genuine, individualized safety risk — never a blanket policy applied
  without regard to the specific person and job.

### 4.2 Anti-patterns

- Writing the essential-functions list only after a specific candidate's accommodation need comes
  up (reactive, evidentially weak, and looks like pretext).
- Asking "can you lift 50 lbs, and do you have any conditions that would prevent that?" in one
  breath — the first half is fine pre-offer, the second half is not, ever, pre-offer.
- Relying on "other duties as assigned" as if it were itself an essential function.

## 5. Pay wording doctrine

### 5.1 The core rule: never "X or Y"

**[KB-HPOST-026]** **Lead with the guaranteed floor. Never present a compensation structure as "X
or Y."** A comp package with any guaranteed component (base hourly, base salary, a guaranteed
minimum draw) must state that guaranteed figure first, using language like "guaranteed" or "at
least," before any variable upside (commission, bonus, production pay, tips) is mentioned. Frame
every variable component strictly as *additional upside on top of* the floor — never as an
alternative path ("$45/hour **or** 30% of production" reads to a candidate as instability and
downside risk — as if the two numbers are competing possibilities rather than a base-plus-bonus
structure) (internal doctrine, incident: a company offering a base-plus-production comp structure
lost a string of qualified candidates in a row to a competitor after presenting the offer as
"$X/hour or Y% of production" — candidates heard this as "you might only get the lower number, and
we're not sure which," not as a guaranteed floor with upside. Re-offering the same total
compensation with a flat guaranteed-floor framing ("$X/hour, guaranteed, plus Y% of production as a
bonus") closed candidates who had declined the "or" framing days earlier with no change to the
actual dollar amounts).

**[KB-HPOST-027]** This rule applies to every candidate-facing surface — the job post itself,
outreach messages, interview conversations, and the offer letter — but not to internal
pay-planning documents, cost sheets, or pro-forma budgets, where the company can and should model
pay however is analytically useful internally (internal doctrine).

**[KB-HPOST-028]** **Decision rule when only a variable/percentage figure exists with no floor:**
if a role genuinely has no guaranteed base at all (pure commission, pure piece rate), do not
manufacture a fake floor — but do not draft external comp language using "or" framing either.
Instead, frame it honestly around realistic expected earnings ("average earners on this role made
$X-$Y last year based on Z production," cited to the company's own real, current numbers — never
invented) and flag to the operator/hiring manager that a role with zero guaranteed floor is a
harder sell to most candidates and may warrant reconsidering the pay structure before drafting the
post (internal doctrine).

### 5.2 Pay type mechanics

**[KB-HPOST-029]** **Hourly.** State the actual hourly rate or range. Under federal law (FLSA),
non-exempt employees must be paid at least the applicable minimum wage and overtime (1.5x the
regular rate) for hours worked beyond 40 in a workweek; an employee is entitled to whichever is
higher between the federal and the state minimum wage where they differ (official — DOL, Wage and
Hour Division, FLSA overview, `dol.gov/agencies/whd/flsa`, retrieved 2026-09-28). A posting for an
hourly, non-exempt role should never imply the rate is "up to" overtime-exempt treatment unless the
role genuinely qualifies for an exemption (executive, administrative, or professional exemptions
each have their own salary-level and duties tests) — an attorney or a current DOL exemption-test
reference should confirm exemption status before a role is posted or paid as exempt.

**[KB-HPOST-030]** **Salary.** State the actual annual salary or range, and confirm the role's
exemption status (exempt vs. non-exempt) is factually correct before publishing — misclassifying a
role as salaried-exempt when it does not meet the duties/salary-level test is a real legal exposure
independent of anything about the job posting itself (official — same DOL source as claim 029,
retrieved 2026-09-28; the specific current exempt salary threshold is a fast-moving number this
file does not restate — verify current the DOL's own overtime rule page before relying on a
specific dollar figure).

**[KB-HPOST-031]** **Commission.** Never post a "commission-only" or heavily commission-weighted
role using "or" framing against a base. If there is a guaranteed base or draw, lead with it per
§5.1. If genuinely commission-only, see the decision rule in claim 028.

**[KB-HPOST-032]** **Piece rate.** State the per-unit rate and give a realistic example of typical
weekly/monthly earnings at a normal pace, sourced from the company's own real production data —
never a best-case number presented as typical.

**[KB-HPOST-033]** **Bonus and tip language.** State bonus eligibility and its actual structure
(sign-on, retention, performance) as upside on top of the stated floor, never blended into the
headline pay figure in a way that makes the guaranteed number look higher than it is. Where tips are
a real part of expected earnings (service roles), state the base wage clearly and describe tips
as a separate, variable addition — do not fold an assumed tip average into a stated "hourly rate"
without clearly labeling it as an estimate that includes tips, since several states restrict or
prohibit counting tips toward a "tip credit" against minimum wage differently — this is a
state-specific mechanic (tip credit rules) that an attorney or current DOL/state wage guidance
should confirm before publishing (internal doctrine, applying the same "state law varies" flag as
§6).

### 5.3 Decision rules — pay wording

- Any guaranteed component exists → lead with it, using "guaranteed" or "at least" in the first
  sentence describing pay.
- Any variable component (commission, bonus, tips, production pay) → frame strictly as additional
  upside, never as an alternative ("or") to the guaranteed figure.
- No guaranteed component exists at all → do not fabricate one; use real average-earnings framing
  instead and flag the harder-sell risk internally.
- Every pay figure that appears externally must trace back to a real, operator-approved number —
  never invented or rounded up to sound more attractive.

### 5.4 Anti-patterns

- "$X/hour or Y% commission" — the exact framing that reads as instability regardless of the
  actual dollar math.
- Quoting a "typical" or "average" earnings figure that is actually a best-case or top-performer
  number.
- Blending tips or bonus into a headline "hourly rate" without labeling the blend.
- Posting a role as salaried-exempt without confirming it actually meets the exemption test.

## 6. State pay-transparency laws requiring pay ranges and benefit descriptions

**[KB-HPOST-034]** A growing number of US states require employers to disclose a pay range (and in
several states, a general benefits description) directly in job postings. This is not a uniform
national rule — coverage, thresholds, and exact required content vary by state, and this list is
not exhaustive. **An attorney should confirm current requirements for any state the company
actually posts jobs in or hires remote workers from** before finalizing a posting template,
because these laws change frequently and some apply based on where the job *could* be performed,
not just where the company is headquartered.

**[KB-HPOST-035]** **Colorado — Equal Pay for Equal Work Act (EPEWA).** Colorado's law is the
broadest of the states covered here: it applies to **all employers, public or private, that employ
at least one person in Colorado** (no minimum size threshold) (official — Colorado Department of
Labor and Employment (CDLE), INFO #9A, "Transparency in Pay & Job Opportunities: Colorado Equal Pay
for Equal Work Act, Part 2,"
`https://cdle.colorado.gov/sites/cdle/files/info_%239a_transparency_in_pay_and_job_opportunities_the_colorado_epewa_part_2_05.29.2024.pdf`,
retrieved 2026-09-28). Every job posting must disclose the pay rate or range, a general description
of other compensation (bonuses, commission), a general description of benefits, **how and by what
deadline to apply — every posting must state an application deadline; "open until filled" does not
comply** (an exception exists for a role that is genuinely continuously accepting applications to
keep up with turnover, which then has no deadline to list), and the pay range must span the actual
lowest-to-highest amount the employer believes it will pay — open-ended ranges like "$30,000 and up"
are not compliant (official, same INFO #9A source, retrieved 2026-09-28). **Two more EPEWA
obligations beyond the posting itself:** the employer must make reasonable efforts to notify all its
Colorado employees of every job opportunity on the same calendar day the opportunity is posted
externally or otherwise made known, before or on the same day a candidate is selected; and, after a
candidate is hired, the employer must send a post-selection notice, within 30 days of the hire, to
every employee the new hire will regularly work with (official, same INFO #9A source, retrieved
2026-09-28). **Colorado also bars using an applicant's credit history** — credit reports,
bankruptcy records, and similar — in a hiring decision unless credit information is substantially
related to the job (official — CDLE INFO #9D, "Using Credit History in Hiring under the Employment
Opportunity Act,"
`https://cdle.colorado.gov/sites/cdle/files/info_%239d_the_colorado_employment_opportunity_act_4.8.26.pdf`,
retrieved 2026-09-28). The prior version of this entry cited CDLE's INFO #9 (an older, combined
Part 1 & Part 2 document at

**[KB-HPOST-036]** **California — SB 1162 (Labor Code § 432.3).** Applies to employers with **15
or more employees**, with at least one employee currently located in California. Requires the
"good faith" salary or hourly wage range the employer reasonably expects to pay, in every job
posting including postings made through a third party; the pay scale must be included directly in
the posting — a link or QR code pointing to the range elsewhere does not satisfy the requirement.
"Pay scale" means a good-faith estimate of the salary or hourly wage range the employer reasonably
expects to pay for the position **upon hire** (Lab. Code § 432.3(m)(1), as amended by SB 642,
effective January 1, 2026). Bonuses, commissions, tips, and other benefits are not required to be
included in the pay-scale figure itself, though the company's own benefits summary elsewhere in the
posting is good practice regardless. The employer must keep a record of each employee's job title
and wage-rate history for the duration of that employee's employment plus three years after it
ends, open to inspection by the Labor Commissioner; failing to keep these records creates a
rebuttable presumption against the employer if a violation is alleged. Violations carry civil
penalties from $100 to $10,000 per violation, enforced by the California Labor Commissioner's
Office, **except that no penalty is assessed for a first violation of the posting requirement if the
employer demonstrates that all job postings for open positions have since been updated to include
the required pay scale** (official — Cal. Lab. Code § 432.3, `leginfo.legislature.ca.gov`, retrieved
2026-09-28).

**[KB-HPOST-037]** **Washington — Equal Pay and Opportunities Act (EPOA).** Applies to employers
with **15 or more employees**, including out-of-state employees, provided the employer has at
least one Washington-based employee; covers postings for jobs that could be filled by a
Washington-based worker, including remote roles. Requires a wage scale or salary range and a
general description of benefits and other compensation in every posting; acceptable ranges look
like "$50,000-$75,000/year" or "$16-$20/hour" — open-ended ranges are not compliant (official —
Washington State Department of Labor & Industries' own Q&A page,
`lni.wa.gov/workers-rights/wages/equal-pay-opportunities-act/`, retrieved 2026-09-28). A 2025
amendment (SB 5408) clarified the required substance of postings, adjusted potential statutory
damages, gave employers a chance to correct a non-compliant posting before being penalized, and
allows listing a single fixed pay amount (instead of a range) when only one amount is actually
offered, including for internal transfers (official, same L&I Q&A source, retrieved 2026-09-28,
corroborated by Ogletree and Morgan Lewis law-firm summaries of SB 5408, retrieved 2026-09-28).

**[KB-HPOST-038]** **New York — Labor Law § 194-b.** Applies to employers with **four or more
employees**. Requires a job description (where one exists) and a compensation range — either the
minimum-to-maximum annual salary or the minimum-to-maximum hourly rate the employer believes in
good faith it will pay at the time of posting — for job postings, promotions, and transfers;
applies to jobs that will be physically performed at least in part in New York, and to remote jobs
that report to a New York-based supervisor or office. **The posting must also clearly state if the
position is commission-based** (official, same NY DOL source, retrieved 2026-09-28). Retaliation
against an employee or applicant who requests this information is prohibited. Civil penalties run
up to $1,000 for a first
violation, up to $2,000 for a second, and up to $3,000 for subsequent violations, enforced by the
New York State Department of Labor's Division of Labor Standards (official — NY Department of
Labor, "Pay Transparency," `dol.ny.gov/pay-transparency`, retrieved 2026-09-28, for the
four-or-more-employee threshold and required-disclosure content; the specific penalty figures are
third-party — multiple law-firm summaries converge on the same numbers, retrieved 2026-09-28 —
confirm the exact current penalty schedule with dol.ny.gov or counsel before quoting it as final).

**[KB-HPOST-039]** **Other states vary.** Beyond the four states detailed above, a number of other
states and some cities (examples reported in third-party legal-industry trackers as of this
writing include Illinois, Maryland, Minnesota, Hawaii, and various city-level ordinances such as
in New York City and Cincinnati) have their own pay-transparency or salary-history requirements
with their own thresholds and mechanics. This file does not attempt to enumerate all of them —
**treat "does our posting need a pay range in this state" as a fresh lookup for every state a
company actually posts a job that could be performed in**, not an assumption carried over from the
four states above (UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation for any state not named in claims 035-038;
the pattern that most such laws key off physical work location, not company headquarters, is
`(internal doctrine)` and generalizes reliably even where the specific state's exact threshold is
unverified).

### 6.1 Decision rules — pay-transparency compliance

- Before publishing any posting, ask: in which state(s) could this job actually be performed
  (including fully remote roles that could be filled from any state)?
- If any of those states is CO, CA, WA, or NY (or another state/city with its own law — verify
  fresh per claim 039), include a real pay range (not an aspirational one) and a general benefits
  description directly in the posting text, not behind a link or QR code.
- Never post an open-ended range ("$50k and up") in a state that requires a bounded range.
- When uncertain which state's law applies to a remote or multi-state role, default to including
  the pay range and benefits description anyway — disclosing more than a specific state strictly
  requires is not itself a violation of that state's own pay-transparency law (internal doctrine —
  this does not clear every OTHER legal question a wider disclosure might raise; it also serves the
  pay-wording doctrine in §5).
- In Colorado, never request or use an applicant's credit history (credit reports, bankruptcy
  records) in a hiring decision unless an attorney confirms credit is substantially related to the
  specific role (`[KB-HPOST-035]`, CDLE INFO #9D) — treat the same default as a reasonable posture
  for other states until each is checked, since a comparable credit-check restriction is common
  though not verified here for California, Washington, or New York specifically.

### 6.2 Anti-patterns

- Assuming a company's home-state law is the only one that matters for a remote posting.
- Treating the four states in this file as the complete list of states with pay-transparency laws.
- Publishing a range so wide it functions as no disclosure at all ("$40,000-$150,000") — several
  states' guidance treats an unrealistically wide range as non-compliant in substance even if
  technically bounded (third-party pattern observed across multiple state summaries above,
  retrieved 2026-09-28 — the company's own good-faith range should reflect what it would actually
  pay a real candidate for the role as posted, not a wide hedge).
- Relying on this file instead of a current, state-specific legal check before a company's first
  posting in a new state.

## Decision rules (summary, cross-referencing all sections)

1. Sort every candidate criterion into must-have (genuinely job-related and consistent with business
   necessity, or a legal credential) or nice-to-have — never invent a third "soft" category that
   functions as an identity screen.
2. Never mention age, sex, national origin, religion, or disability status, directly or through
   coded language, anywhere in a posting.
3. Describe every physical or cognitive job demand as a specific, measurable task, never a vague
   health trait — this serves both EEO and ADA compliance at once.
4. Write essential functions before posting, not reactively after a specific candidate's need
   surfaces.
5. Every comp figure leads with its guaranteed floor; every variable component is framed as
   upside, never as an "or" alternative.
6. Every posting includes a real pay range and benefits summary if the job could be performed in a
   state that requires it (currently confirmed: CO, CA, WA, NY — verify fresh for any other state).
7. When in doubt on any legal question in this file, the answer is "ask an employment attorney,"
   not "guess and publish."

## Anti-patterns (summary)

- A requirements list padded with unnecessary "nice to haves" mislabeled as must-haves.
- Any coded language for a protected characteristic, including "culture fit," "digital native," or
  an unstated age/gender preference.
- "$X or Y%" comp framing.
- Posting without checking whether the job's location(s) trigger a pay-transparency disclosure
  requirement.
- Treating any of the above as settled without a current, direct read of the primary source when
  a real decision — not just an internal draft — is on the line.
