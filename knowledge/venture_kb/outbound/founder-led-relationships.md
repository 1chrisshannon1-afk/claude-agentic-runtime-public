# Founder-Led Selling and Relationship Building

**What this file answers:** how does a solo, first-time founder — with no sales team, no
marketing budget, and often no prior sales experience — build the relationships that produce a
tiny company's first customers, partners, and champions, by hand, compliantly, and without
pretending to be bigger than they are. This is the "do it yourself, in person or one-to-one"
counterpart to
[`b2b-cold-email-and-deliverability.md`](b2b-cold-email-and-deliverability.md)'s compliant-at-scale
outbound channel. This file's claim ids use the `[KB-VOUT-NNN]` prefix, continuing the numbering
from that file (starting at 025) so ids stay unique within the two `outbound/` files together.

---

## 1. Design-partner programs

**[KB-VOUT-025] (internal doctrine)** A "design partner" is an early customer (or prospective
customer) who agrees to use an early, unfinished, or beta version of a product in exchange for
close involvement in shaping it — direct input on the roadmap, early/discounted or free access, and
often a closer support relationship than a normal customer gets. For a solo founder with no
product-market fit yet, a design-partner program is usually a better first move than a broad sales
motion: a handful of design partners who will actually give unvarnished feedback is worth more than
a larger number of early signups who never engage.

**[KB-VOUT-026] (internal doctrine)** A non-binding design-partner letter (sometimes called a
"letter of intent" for a design partnership, though it should be clearly distinguished from a
binding LOI used in an M&A or financing context — see the venture department's capital/exit doctrine
for that separate use of the term) typically covers, in plain language:
1. **What the partner gets:** access terms (free, discounted, or standard pricing after a defined
   period), the support/responsiveness level committed to them, and — if true — some acknowledgment
   that their specific use case will inform the roadmap (never promise a *specific* feature will
   ship by a specific date; promise attention and consideration, not a committed deliverable, unless
   the founder is prepared to actually commit to that deliverable in writing).
2. **What the founder is asking for:** a defined engagement level (e.g., a certain number of
   feedback sessions per month, willingness to be a reference/case study later if the relationship
   goes well, early adoption of new features to test them).
3. **Explicitly non-binding language:** that the letter is a statement of mutual intent, not a
   contract, creates no payment obligation beyond what's separately agreed, and either party can end
   the relationship at any time. This is the feature that makes a design-partner letter fast to send
   and sign — it is deliberately not a negotiated commercial contract.
4. **Confidentiality, if the partner will see pre-release features or non-public data:** a short
   mutual confidentiality clause, or a reference to a separate short-form NDA if the relationship
   involves seeing genuinely sensitive information.
5. **What it explicitly does NOT do:** it does not obligate the partner to buy anything, does not
   grant the founder rights to use the partner's name/logo/case-study details publicly without
   separate written permission, and does not create an exclusivity arrangement in either direction
   unless that is a deliberate, separately-flagged term both sides actually intend.
A design-partner letter is a business document, not a court-tested legal instrument — a founder
should still have an attorney glance at the template once before reusing it across multiple
partners, particularly the confidentiality and non-binding language, but it does not need
per-partner legal review once a template is vetted. `(internal doctrine)`, not independently sourced
to a specific published template — this reflects common early-stage practice, not a citation to an
official standard-form document.

**[KB-VOUT-027] (internal doctrine)** A design-partner relationship that quietly becomes a real,
paying, contractually-bound customer relationship (the product ships, they're paying full or
discounted price on an ongoing basis, they depend on it operationally) has outgrown the
non-binding-letter stage. At that point, put a real, if simple, customer agreement in place —
continuing to operate a revenue-generating, operationally-depended-upon relationship on a letter
that says "either party can end this at any time" and "creates no obligations" is a mismatch between
the paper and the reality that will surface badly if something goes wrong later (a dispute, a
service outage, a pricing change).

## 2. Referral and channel partners

**[KB-VOUT-028] (internal doctrine)** For a small local or vertical software business, the highest-
leverage referral partners are often businesses that already have a trusted relationship with the
exact buyer the founder is trying to reach but do not compete with the founder's own product:
- **IT providers / managed service providers (MSPs)** serving the target industry, who are already
  trusted with technology recommendations.
- **Bookkeepers and accountants** serving the target industry, who see a prospective customer's
  operational pain points (the same spreadsheet-and-duct-tape problems a software product often
  solves) before almost anyone else does.
- **Consultants** who advise the target industry on operations, growth, or compliance, and are
  regularly asked "what tools do you recommend."
None of these are direct competitors to a product-focused founder, which is what makes them durable
referral sources rather than one-off favors.

**[KB-VOUT-029] (internal doctrine)** A referral-fee structure for this kind of partner is typically
one of: (a) a flat one-time fee per referred customer that converts to paying, (b) a percentage of
first-year (or first-contract) revenue from the referred customer, or (c) a recurring
percentage-of-revenue share for as long as the referred customer remains a paying customer. Simpler
(flat fee or first-year percentage) is usually better for a solo founder just starting a referral
program: a recurring revenue-share arrangement is harder to track accurately over time, creates an
ongoing accounting/payment obligation that a one-person company may not have the operational
infrastructure to administer cleanly, and is harder to unwind if the partnership isn't working out.

**[KB-VOUT-030] (internal doctrine)** A referral-fee arrangement needs actual legal review, not just
a handshake or an emailed summary, when any of the following is true: (a) the fee is a **recurring
revenue share** rather than a one-time flat payment (creates an ongoing contractual and
tax-reporting relationship); (b) the referral partner is themselves a **licensed professional**
(accountant, attorney, insurance agent, real-estate agent) whose own licensing/professional-conduct
rules may restrict or regulate referral compensation — many licensed professions have specific rules
about accepting referral fees, and violating them is the partner's professional risk but the
founder's reputational and contractual risk too; (c) the arrangement will be **formalized in a
written contract** at all, as opposed to an informal, no-payment-obligation "let each other know
about opportunities" relationship; or (d) the volume/dollar value crosses a threshold where a
dispute would meaningfully hurt the business (a founder's own judgment call, but "we're now paying
this partner five figures a year" is well past the point of needing a real reviewed agreement). A
**simple, informal, no-formal-fee mutual-referral relationship** ("I'll mention you, you mention me,
no money changes hands") generally does not need legal review to start — it needs review the moment
money or a written commitment enters the picture.

## 3. Trade associations and events

**[KB-VOUT-031] (internal doctrine)** For a vertical software product serving a specific
industry (contractors, dental practices, any licensed trade), the industry's own trade
associations and their events are typically the highest-density concentration of the exact buyer
persona in one room, self-selected for actually caring about the trade's problems. A founder's
practical playbook: (a) identify the 1–3 associations most relevant to the target vertical
(national or regional — a regional/state chapter is often more accessible and less expensive for a
first-time founder than a national conference); (b) attend as a participant before ever trying to
sponsor or exhibit — this builds real relationships and product intuition cheaply, before spending
real money on a booth; (c) look for association-run vendor/partner programs, which sometimes carry
implicit credibility with members ("recommended by [association]") that a cold pitch could never
buy, but verify what that endorsement actually costs and requires before committing to it; (d) treat
one-on-one conversations at an event as the real product, not the booth or the swag — a solo founder
without a marketing budget wins by being genuinely useful and interesting to talk to, not by
out-spending competitors on event presence.

**[KB-VOUT-032] (internal doctrine)** A trade association's own marketing/vendor rules (what a
non-member vendor can and can't do at an event, whether direct solicitation on the show floor
outside a paid booth is allowed, whether attendee list access is sold and under what use
restrictions) vary by association and are not generalizable here — check each specific
association's own exhibitor/attendee rules before an event, rather than assuming norms from one
association carry to another.

## 4. LinkedIn by hand — the no-automation rule

**[KB-VOUT-033]** LinkedIn's User Agreement, § 8.2 ("Don'ts"), prohibits developing, supporting, or
using "software, devices, scripts, robots or any other means or processes (such as crawlers,
browser plugins and add-ons or any other technology) to scrape or copy the Services" and separately
prohibits using "bots or other unauthorized automated methods to access the Services, add or
download contacts, send or redirect messages, create, comment on, like, share, or re-share posts"
(official, LinkedIn User Agreement §8.2, retrieved 2026-09-28,
https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement). This directly rules out the entire category of
"LinkedIn automation" tools marketed for auto-connecting, auto-messaging, auto-liking, or scraping
profile/contact data — regardless of how a given tool markets itself as "safe" or "undetectable,"
using one is a User Agreement violation that risks account restriction or termination.

**[KB-VOUT-034] (internal doctrine)** For a solo founder, "LinkedIn by hand" means: the founder (or
someone at the company, using their own real account) personally writes and sends connection
requests and messages, personally reviews and engages with content, and personally exports or
records only what LinkedIn's own interface displays to them as a normal user — no third-party tool
reads, writes, or automates any part of the account. This is slower than automation by design, and
that is the tradeoff: a smaller number of higher-quality, personally-written outreach messages
reliably outperforms a larger volume of templated automated ones in relationship-building context
anyway, so the compliance constraint and the actual best practice point the same direction here.

**[KB-VOUT-035] (internal doctrine)** A research assistant (a human or an AI agent) may help *draft*
LinkedIn messages or *identify* candidates/prospects from information already gathered through
normal, manual, logged-in browsing — but must never be the one to actually log into LinkedIn and
click send, connect, or scrape on the founder's behalf via any automated or scripted process. The
distinction is who/what performs the actual action inside LinkedIn's interface, not who drafted the
words.

## 5. Facebook groups — as yourself

**[KB-VOUT-036] (internal doctrine)** Industry-specific Facebook groups (a regional contractors'
group, a vertical professional group) are a real, low-cost relationship channel for a founder
willing to participate authentically over time — answering questions, sharing genuinely useful
information, and only occasionally and transparently mentioning the founder's own product when it's
actually relevant to the question being discussed. The failure mode to avoid: joining a group
purely to pitch, posting promotional content with no participation history, or posting as if a
company/brand rather than as the actual person the group members would recognize — most such groups
have explicit rules against undisclosed self-promotion, and even where a rule isn't explicit,
members recognize and resent a drive-by pitch pattern quickly, which burns the channel for good
rather than just failing once.

**[KB-VOUT-037] (internal doctrine)** Post as the actual person (real name, real profile), never as
a page, alias, or someone else's account — this is both a Facebook platform-integrity expectation
for group participation and the only way relationship-based credibility in a community group
actually accrues. If a group's own rules require disclosure when a member has a commercial interest
in what they're posting about, follow that disclosure rule explicitly rather than relying on "it's
obvious from my profile" — a first-time founder should assume group moderators and members notice
and object to undisclosed self-interest fast.

## 6. Discovery-call structure

**[KB-VOUT-038] (internal doctrine)** For a first-time founder without formal sales training, a
simple, repeatable discovery-call structure prevents the two most common founder-led-sales failure
modes: talking only about the product (never learning what the prospect actually needs) and
talking only about the prospect's problem (never getting to an actual next step). A workable
default shape for a 20–30 minute call:
1. **Open (2–3 min):** confirm the time available, state the call's purpose in one sentence, ask
   permission to ask a few questions before describing the product.
2. **Discover (10–15 min):** ask open-ended questions about the prospect's current process, what's
   working, what's frustrating, and what they've tried before (including competitors or workarounds)
   — listen far more than talk in this phase; the founder's job here is understanding, not pitching.
3. **Connect (5–8 min):** describe only the parts of the product that map directly to what the
   prospect just described — never the full feature list. If something the prospect needs isn't
   built yet, say so plainly rather than implying it exists; a first-time founder's early customers
   are disproportionately also potential design partners and references, and losing their trust with
   an oversold pitch costs far more than losing the individual deal.
4. **Next step (2–3 min):** propose a specific, concrete next step (a trial, a demo of a specific
   workflow, a design-partner conversation, a proposal) with a specific date — never end a call with
   only "I'll follow up," which defaults to no forward motion.

**[KB-VOUT-039] (internal doctrine)** Never claim on a discovery call, or in any founder-led sales
conversation, a feature that doesn't exist, a customer count or case study that isn't real, or a
timeline commitment the founder isn't actually prepared to honor. For a first-time founder with few
or no existing customers, the honest framing ("you'd be one of our first customers, and here's
specifically what that means for you — closer attention, input on the roadmap, and [whatever the
real tradeoff is]") is both the legally/ethically correct approach and, in this corpus's experience
across adjacent domains, a more effective one with the kind of early-adopter buyer who actually
converts for a brand-new company — that buyer is often explicitly looking for the closer
relationship a tiny company can offer, not pretending the company is bigger than it is.

## 7. Follow-up discipline

**[KB-VOUT-040]** The general follow-up-cadence doctrine (touch counts, spacing, when a lead is
legitimately dead) is owned by the sales domain, not restated here — see
[`../../marketing_kb/sales/follow-up-cadences.md`](../../marketing_kb/sales/follow-up-cadences.md).
What's specific to founder-led relationship building (as opposed to a scaled outbound motion) is
discipline under low volume: with only a handful of active relationships at a time, a solo founder
has no automated safety net catching a dropped follow-up, and the personal-relationship nature of
this channel means a dropped follow-up reads as a broken promise, not a missed automated touch.
`(internal doctrine)`: track every open relationship (design-partner conversations, referral-partner
touches, discovery-call next steps) in one place (see §8) with an explicit next-action date, and
review that list at a fixed daily or every-other-day cadence — the discipline that matters here is
the review habit, not a specific tool.

## 8. CRM-lite tracking for relationships

**[KB-VOUT-041] (internal doctrine)** A solo founder juggling design partners, referral partners,
association contacts, and discovery-call prospects does not need a full CRM platform to start —
but does need *something* more durable than memory or scattered email threads. A minimal
"CRM-lite" that scales from zero: one spreadsheet or lightweight tracker with a row per relationship
carrying, at minimum: contact name/company, relationship type (design partner / referral partner /
prospect / association contact), last touch date, next planned action + date, and a one-line status
note. The specific tool (a spreadsheet, a free-tier lightweight CRM, a notes app with good search)
matters far less than the discipline of actually updating it after every meaningful touch — the
general CRM-hygiene doctrine (required fields, decay triggers, pipeline-stage definitions) scales up
from this same discipline once volume grows; see
[`../../marketing_kb/sales/crm-hygiene.md`](../../marketing_kb/sales/crm-hygiene.md) for the fuller
treatment once the relationship volume outgrows a spreadsheet.

## 9. The hard lines

**[KB-VOUT-042]** No automated or AI-voice calls, no ringless voicemail, and no cold texts to
anyone the founder does not already have channel-specific written consent from — this is a
this-corpus policy that bans cold texts and automated/AI-voice outreach entirely, stricter than the
TCPA floor itself requires in every case, applied uniformly regardless of how personal or small-scale
the outreach is. See [`../../marketing_kb/compliance/tcpa-a2p-10dlc.md`](../../marketing_kb/compliance/tcpa-a2p-10dlc.md)
for the full doctrine (statutory damages of $500 per violation, trebled to $1,500 for a willful
violation — [KB-COMPLIANCE-026] — and the channel-separateness and any-reasonable-means-revocation
rules). **The TCPA's own exposure, precisely stated (47 U.S.C. §227, read directly for this pass):**
the §227(b) consent requirement — the one carrying the $500/$1,500-per-violation exposure cited above
— applies specifically to a call or text made using an "automatic telephone dialing system" or an
"artificial or prerecorded voice," not to every unconsented call or text as a category; a single such
autodialed or prerecorded/AI-voice call or text without the required consent is genuinely a single
violation, exposed the same as if a large company sent it. Separately, §227(c)(5)'s do-not-call
private right of action requires "more than one telephone call within any 12-month period by or on
behalf of the same entity" before it is actionable — a single ordinary, human-dialed call to one
person without an autodialer or prerecorded/AI voice is not, on its own, a TCPA §227(c) violation
under the statute's own private-right-of-action text. **None of this loosens this corpus's own
policy** — the founder-led-relationships doctrine in this file bans cold texts and automated/AI-voice
outreach as a matter of this corpus's own risk posture, not because every instance would otherwise be
a guaranteed statutory violation; state it as this corpus's stricter policy choice, not as "any
unconsented call or text is automatically a TCPA violation," which overstates the statute.

**[KB-VOUT-043] (internal doctrine)** The channels this file documents (design-partner letters,
referral partnerships, association/event relationships, hand-driven LinkedIn and Facebook-group
participation, discovery calls) are, not coincidentally, all channels where a real human is doing
the reaching-out personally, in a context where the recipient has some reasonable expectation of
being contacted (a professional association, a business networking context, a warm introduction).
That is what keeps them outside TCPA's autodialer/prerecorded-voice trigger and outside CAN-SPAM's
bulk-commercial-email mechanics in the first place — the moment any of these channels is
automated, scaled, or scripted (an auto-dialer, a bulk AI-voice call campaign, a scraped contact
list blasted with a script), it stops being "founder-led relationship building" and becomes a
different, more heavily regulated activity that this file's doctrine does not cover — see
[`b2b-cold-email-and-deliverability.md`](b2b-cold-email-and-deliverability.md) for the compliant
version of doing outreach at that scale instead.

## Decision rules

1. **Is this an early customer being asked to use unfinished/beta product in exchange for input and
   early access?** → Use a non-binding design-partner letter, never a full commercial contract at
   this stage (§1).
2. **Has a design-partner relationship become a real, paying, depended-upon relationship?** → Move
   to a real (if simple) customer agreement; stop relying on "non-binding, cancel anytime" language
   once the relationship isn't actually that anymore (§1).
3. **Is a referral-fee arrangement a recurring revenue share, involves a licensed professional, will
   be a written contract, or involves meaningful dollar volume?** → Get real legal review before
   formalizing it (§2). An informal, no-money, mutual-mention arrangement does not need review to
   start.
4. **Does the task involve connecting, messaging, liking, commenting, or extracting data on
   LinkedIn via any tool other than a human logged in and clicking?** → Refuse; §8.2 of the User
   Agreement prohibits it outright, and a human-drafted, human-sent message is both compliant and
   usually more effective anyway (§4).
5. **Is the next planned outreach step an automated call, prerecorded/AI voice, ringless voicemail,
   or a cold text?** → Stop; these are never in scope for founder-led relationship building
   regardless of scale or personal framing (§9).
6. **Is a Facebook-group post being drafted as a brand/page voice, or with no participation history
   in the group?** → Rewrite as the real person, and check the group's own rules on commercial
   disclosure before posting anything with a commercial angle (§5).
7. **Does a discovery-call draft or founder talking point include a feature that doesn't exist yet,
   or a customer count/case study that isn't real?** → Remove it; honest "you'd be one of our first
   customers" framing outperforms an oversold pitch with this buyer type anyway (§6).

## Anti-patterns

- Using any LinkedIn automation/scraping tool, no matter how it's marketed, because "everyone does
  it" — §8.2 of the User Agreement bans it outright and the account-level risk (restriction,
  termination) falls entirely on the founder (§4).
- Drafting an automated, scripted, or AI-voice outreach call or a cold text and calling it
  "founder-led" because the founder approved the script — automation is exactly the line that moves
  an activity out of this file's doctrine and into TCPA's most heavily regulated category (§9).
- Formalizing a recurring-revenue-share referral-fee arrangement with a licensed professional on a
  handshake, with no legal review, because "it's just an informal thing for now" — the moment it's
  written down and recurring, it needs review (§2).
- Posting in an industry Facebook group as a page/brand, or joining a group only to pitch with no
  participation history — burns the channel and often violates the group's own rules (§5).
- Overselling on a discovery call — claiming a feature, customer count, or timeline that isn't real
  — to close a first customer who is disproportionately likely to become a reference or design
  partner the founder needs to trust them later (§6).
- Letting a design-partner relationship quietly become a real paying dependency while still
  operating under "non-binding, cancel anytime" paper, with nothing written down about what happens
  if the relationship sours (§1).
- Tracking relationships only in memory or scattered email threads with no next-action date anywhere
  — the personal-relationship nature of this channel means a dropped follow-up reads as a broken
  promise (§7, §8).
