# Selling a local, owner-operated service business (remodeling, restoration, and similar trades)

**What this file answers:** how a small, owner-operated home-services business (residential
remodeling, restoration, general contracting, and similar licensed trades) is actually valued when
it is sold as a whole company; which levers raise or lower the multiple a buyer will pay; how SBA
7(a) buyer financing shapes what a deal can look like; what happens to the owner's trade license
and insurance on a change of ownership; what a serious buyer will diligence; and how rebuilding the
marketing and lead engine before going to market changes the price a buyer is willing to pay. It is
written for a first-time owner selling one local trade business, not a platform/roll-up deal or a
company already carrying institutional financials.

## 1. How a small local-service business is valued

**The standard currency is Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE), not EBITDA, until a business
clears roughly $2M in sale price.** SDE starts from the business's reported net income (or pre-tax
profit) and adds back: the owner's total compensation and payroll taxes on that compensation,
interest expense, non-cash depreciation and amortization, one-time or non-recurring expenses, and
personal/discretionary expenses the owner ran through the business (a personal vehicle, family
health insurance, above-market rent paid to a related party, etc.) (third-party, Duran Advisors
"What Is SDE? Seller's Discretionary Earnings, Explained for Owners," retrieved 2026-09-28).
The result is meant to represent the total economic benefit one full-time working owner gets from
the business in a year — the number a buyer who will personally run the business actually cares
about, because it is *their* prospective take-home, not a return on invested capital the way
EBITDA is for a financial buyer. [KB-VSALE-001]

**Once a deal price crosses roughly $2M, brokers and buyers typically shift the multiple base from
SDE to EBITDA**, because at that size the buyer is more often a financial buyer or a company doing
its own add-on acquisition, and EBITDA (which does NOT add back a market-rate owner/GM salary) is
the metric that financial buyers and their lenders use. A business in the $1M–$2M price range may
be quoted either way; ask which base a given multiple is quoted against before comparing it to
another deal (internal doctrine, retrieved 2026-09-28 — synthesized from the SDE/EBITDA crossover
point reported across multiple 2026 sources below). [KB-VSALE-002]

### 1.1 SDE and EBITDA multiples by deal-size band (2026 data)

The IBBA (International Business Brokers Association) and M&A Source jointly run a quarterly
**Market Pulse survey** of business brokers and M&A advisors reporting on recently closed
transactions, segmented into five price bands. For Q1 2026 (203 closed transactions reported by
roughly 300 advisors, published May 2026), the median multiples reported by band were:

| Deal price band | Median multiple | Multiple base |
|---|---|---|
| Under $500K | 2.0x | SDE |
| $500K – $1M | 2.8x | SDE |
| $1M – $2M | 3.0x | SDE |
| $2M – $5M | 4.0x | EBITDA |
| $5M – $50M | 4.0x | EBITDA |

(third-party, Duran Advisors "EBITDA Multiples: What Businesses Actually Sold For in 2026,"
retrieved 2026-09-28 — figures extracted from the IBBA/M&A Source Market Pulse Q1 2026 survey PDF;
the primary PDF itself could not be machine-read during this pass, so this table is sourced to a
third party's extraction of it, not to a direct read of the primary document — re-verify against
`ibba.org`'s own Market Pulse release before using this table to anchor a specific asking price).
[KB-VSALE-003]

A separate, independently run market report — **BizBuySell's quarterly Insight Report**, drawn from
its own marketplace listing and closed-sale data — found small-business deal activity fell roughly
10% in Q2 2026, but valuations held steady: the average cash-flow (SDE) multiple for businesses sold
held at **2.65x** between Q1 and Q2 2026, and the median sale price fell only slightly, from roughly
$350K to roughly $349K (third-party, BizBuySell Insight Report data as reported by Sundance
Financial "BizBuySell's Q2 2026 Insight Report: Three Takeaways," retrieved 2026-09-28 via direct
fetch — the cited page does not contain a 1.75x-3.13x interquartile range, a 2.58x cross-sector
average, a "2.7x, up 2% year over year" figure, or a 0.7x revenue multiple; those figures have been
removed as unsupported by the cited source). [KB-VSALE-004]

**Reading these two surveys together:** they use different methodologies and sampling (IBBA/M&A
Source surveys advisors about closed deals across all industries by price band; BizBuySell reports
its own marketplace's listing and closed-sale data by industry category), and they will not match
exactly. Treat both as **directional ranges**, not a formula that produces one correct number. A
specific business's actual multiple is set by the levers in §2 below, applied within whichever band
its trailing SDE falls into — not by picking the midpoint of a survey range and multiplying.
[KB-VSALE-005]

**A remodeling/restoration/general-contracting business selling under roughly $1M in price** should
expect to be priced in the **2.0x–2.8x SDE** range as a starting anchor before the multiple is
adjusted up or down for the specific factors in §2 — with genuinely below-average risk factors
(heavy owner dependence, no license held by a non-owner, thin insurance history, high customer
concentration) pushing toward the bottom of that range or below it, and genuinely above-average
factors (see §2.2) pushing toward the top of the band or into the next band up. **(internal doctrine,
retrieved 2026-09-28 — this anchor is derived from the IBBA/M&A Source table's Under-$500K and
$500K-$1M SDE-multiple bands above, not itself a separately cited statistic)** [KB-VSALE-006]

### 1.2 Revenue multiples are a weaker signal for this trade class

A revenue multiple (price ÷ annual revenue) is sometimes quoted casually but is a poor primary
metric for a remodeling/restoration business, because gross margin and owner-dependence vary so
widely between two contractors with identical top-line revenue — a $2M-revenue company clearing
$150K in owner benefit and one clearing $500K are not comparable on revenue alone. `UNVERIFIED —
seeded, needs derivation`: this file previously cited a BizBuySell average cross-sector revenue
multiple of 0.7x, but that figure does not appear in the cited Sundance Financial source (which
reports only the 2.65x cash-flow multiple and ~$349K median sale price in §1.1) and has been removed
pending a real citation. Treat any specific revenue multiple as needing its own sourced re-derivation
before use; the underlying point — that a revenue multiple is a weak primary metric for this trade
class and should be used only as a sanity check against the SDE-multiple answer, never as the primary
valuation method — still holds regardless of the specific multiple. [KB-VSALE-007]

## 2. What raises and lowers the multiple

The IBBA/M&A Source data and the brokers who report into it consistently name **transferability of
the business's earnings to a new owner** — not raw size — as the dominant driver of where a specific
deal lands within (or outside) its size band: two service businesses with materially similar SDE can
sell at materially different multiples based on the risk factors below (third-party, Duran Advisors,
retrieved 2026-09-28, characterizing the IBBA Market Pulse commentary). [KB-VSALE-008]

### 2.1 Factors that LOWER the multiple

- **Owner dependence.** If the owner personally holds every customer relationship, personally bids
  every job, personally holds the trade license (see §4), and the business has no second person who
  can run a job or a sales call without the owner, a buyer is really buying a job for themselves, not
  a business — and will price accordingly, often demanding the low end of the band or a longer
  transition/consulting period as a condition of closing (third-party, Duran Advisors, retrieved
  2026-09-28). [KB-VSALE-009]
- **Licenses and certifications held only by the owner personally** (state contractor license,
  manufacturer certifications that route leads or warranty work, insurance-carrier "preferred
  vendor" status). If none of these can transfer or be re-qualified by a new owner or an existing
  employee without a gap, a buyer must factor in real time, cost, and risk to become compliant
  before they can legally operate — see §4. [KB-VSALE-010]
- **Insurance status problems** — a lapse in general liability or workers' comp coverage, an
  E&O/liability claim history, or an inability to produce clean certificates of insurance for
  completed jobs, all read as diligence red flags and depress price or kill the deal outright (see
  §5). [KB-VSALE-011]
- **Customer concentration.** A business drawing a large share of revenue from one customer, one
  insurance carrier's referral pipeline, or one general contractor is priced down relative to a
  business with a broad, diversified customer base, because losing that one relationship after
  closing is an outsized risk the buyer inherits (third-party, Duran Advisors, retrieved 2026-09-28).
  [KB-VSALE-012]
- **Related-party revenue.** Revenue that depends on a relationship the seller controls personally
  (a referral arrangement with a friend's company, work performed for the owner's own other
  businesses or property, or jobs priced at other than arm's length) does not survive a change of
  ownership and should not be counted in SDE at face value — a buyer's diligence team will strip it
  out or heavily discount it, and an honest seller should flag and adjust for it before a buyer finds
  it (internal doctrine, retrieved 2026-09-28). [KB-VSALE-013]
- **No documented systems.** If pricing, job costing, scheduling, and safety/quality procedures live
  only in the owner's head, a buyer cannot verify what they are actually buying and cannot safely
  operate on day one — this converts directly into either a lower price or a longer, more
  expensive transition period demanded of the seller. [KB-VSALE-014]
- **Lumpy or unpredictable revenue with no repeat/recurring component.** A business that wins 100%
  of its revenue job-by-job, with nothing recurring (maintenance contracts, warranty-service
  agreements, repeat-customer base with a measurable repeat rate), is priced as pure project risk.
  [KB-VSALE-015]

### 2.2 Factors that RAISE the multiple

- **Recurring or contracted revenue** — maintenance agreements, warranty-service contracts, or a
  measurable repeat/referral customer base — is priced at a premium over one-off project revenue,
  because it is the part of the business most likely to survive a change of ownership intact
  (third-party, Duran Advisors, retrieved 2026-09-28). [KB-VSALE-016]
- **A documented, working lead-generation engine** the owner can hand over — a paid-search or paid-
  social program with a known cost per lead, a website generating organic/local-search leads, a CRM
  with lead source tracked back to booked-job economics — de-risks the single scariest question a
  buyer has about any small service business: "where does next month's revenue come from without
  the seller." See §6. [KB-VSALE-017]
- **Management depth beyond the owner.** A second-in-command who can run estimating, sales, or
  field operations without the owner directly demonstrates that the earnings can transfer to a new
  owner, and is one of the two levers (alongside revenue structure) the third-party source above
  identifies as separating high- and low-multiple outcomes within the same SDE band.
  [KB-VSALE-018]
- **Clean, verifiable financials.** Financials that hold up under diligence — tax returns that
  reconcile to the internal books, invoices and job-costing records that support the reported
  numbers, no unexplained cash revenue — directly raise the multiple a buyer and their lender (if
  SBA-financed, see §3) will support, because unverifiable numbers cannot be underwritten at all
  (third-party, Duran Advisors, retrieved 2026-09-28). [KB-VSALE-019]
- **A license or certification an employee (not just the owner) already holds**, or a documented,
  realistic path for an employee to become the license-holder of record, meaningfully reduces a
  buyer's post-closing compliance risk — see §4. [KB-VSALE-020]

## 3. Buyer financing: SBA 7(a) loans and what they require of the deal structure

The large majority of individual buyers of a business in this price range finance the purchase with
an **SBA 7(a) loan**, because conventional bank financing for a business acquisition with no hard
collateral is hard to get otherwise. SBA lending rules are not optional deal terms — they are
federal program requirements the lender must follow, and a deal structured outside them simply
cannot use SBA financing, which removes most individual buyers from the pool. The controlling
document is the SBA's **Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) 50 10**, currently version **50 10 8**
(effective June 1, 2025), which is superseded by **SOP 50 10 8.1** effective October 1, 2026 — the
50 10 8.1 document itself was read directly (Word document, fetched from legacy.sba.gov) for this
pass, and both versions are cited by section below where their rules differ. [KB-VSALE-021]

### 3.1 Equity injection requirement

For a **complete change of ownership** financed with an SBA 7(a) loan, the buyer must inject a
minimum of **10% of total project cost** in equity. This 10% floor applies to both new-business
startups and complete changes of ownership under SOP 50 10 8 (third-party, multiple concurring
sources: Live Oak Bank "Navigating the SBA's New SOP 50 10 8" and Starfield & Smith "Best Practices:
A Review of Equity Injection Requirements Under SOP 50 10 8," both retrieved 2026-09-28).
[KB-VSALE-022]

### 3.2 Seller notes: standby requirement and the 50% cap

A **seller note** (the seller financing part of the purchase price and carrying it as debt owed by
the buyer) can be used to satisfy part — but never all — of the buyer's required equity injection:

- A seller note may satisfy **no more than 50% of the total required equity injection**. Concretely,
  against a 10%-of-project-cost injection requirement, a seller note can cover at most half of that
  10% (i.e., up to 5% of total project cost), meaning the buyer must still bring the other half (at
  least 5% of total project cost) as genuine unborrowed cash from personal funds. [KB-VSALE-023]
- To count toward the injection at all, the seller note must be on **full standby** — no principal
  and no interest payments — for the **entire term of the SBA loan**, which is typically a 10-year
  term for this kind of acquisition loan. This is documented on a signed **SBA Form 155**
  certifying the standby terms. (third-party, multiple concurring sources — Live Oak Bank, Reins
  "SOP 50 10 8: Seller Notes, Standby and Equity Injection," and windsoradvantage.com, all retrieved
  2026-09-28.) [KB-VSALE-024]
- **Practical consequence for the seller:** if the deal is structured to lean on a seller note for
  financing, the seller should expect to receive **no payments at all on that note** for as long as
  it is required to stay on full standby to satisfy the SBA equity-injection requirement — the note
  is real debt owed to the seller, but it is not a source of the seller's near-term cash. **This is
  not a lifetime-of-the-loan condition, though.** SOP 50 10 8.1 states that seller debt structured in
  conjunction with a 7(a) change-of-ownership transaction "is eligible to be refinanced after it has
  been in place and current for 36 months" — so a seller who is on full standby against a typical
  10-year acquisition loan becomes eligible, after 36 months in place and current, to be paid off from the proceeds of a new
  7(a) loan, subject to SBA's refinancing rules and lender underwriting. Nothing lifts the standby
  agreement itself (SBA Form 155: no principal or interest for the term of the original 7(a) loan):
  unless such a refinancing happens, the standby stays in force for the life of the original loan. **(official,
  SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, effective 2026-10-01, read directly from the .docx published at legacy.sba.gov,
  retrieved 2026-09-28)** [KB-VSALE-025a]. A seller note structured
  *outside* the equity-injection requirement (as additional, non-standby seller financing layered on
  top of the SBA loan and the buyer's own cash injection) is not bound by the same standby rule, but
  changes the deal's overall debt load and the lender's own underwriting of the buyer's ability to
  service all the debt combined — this is a structuring question for the deal's own SBA lender and
  the seller's own advisor to work out, not a generic rule. [KB-VSALE-025]

### 3.3 If the seller stays on with any ownership stake

**The rule splits at 20% retained ownership, not at "any ownership stake":**

- **A selling owner who remains a direct or indirect owner and retains LESS THAN 20% of the business
  post-sale** must provide a **full personal guarantee for the full loan amount**, under 13 CFR
  120.160(a). Under SOP 50 10 8.1, the term of that guaranty must be for **at least two years** after
  final loan disbursement (SBA Form 148L or the lender's equivalent); SBA does not require these
  2-year guarantors to pledge personal assets such as their residence against a collateral shortfall.
- **A selling owner who retains 20% or more of the business post-sale** falls under the standard
  SBA guaranty rules that apply to any owner of 20%+, which is a full, unlimited personal guarantee
  for the life of the loan — not a time-limited 2-year guarantee.

So a seller retaining even a 1% stake and a seller retaining 15% are treated the same (a 2-year
full guarantee), but a seller retaining 20% or more is treated differently and more strictly (a
full-loan-term guarantee) — "1% is the same as 40%" is not correct; the line is drawn at 20%.
**(official, SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, effective 2026-10-01, and 13 CFR 120.160(a), both read directly from
the SOP .docx published at legacy.sba.gov, retrieved 2026-09-28 — third-party sources including Live
Oak Bank and Reins repeat a simplified "even 1%, 2 years, no exceptions" framing that omits the 20%
split; treat the SOP's own text above as controlling)** [KB-VSALE-026]. A seller who wants a clean
exit with no ongoing guarantee obligation should sell 100% of their ownership at closing, not retain
a token equity stake — but a seller planning to retain a meaningful stake (20%+) should not assume
the 2-year guarantee applies to them; confirm which guarantee regime applies with the deal's SBA
lender before assuming either outcome.

### 3.3a No seller earnouts in an SBA-financed deal

Both SOP versions state: "Seller earnouts are prohibited; however, buyer rebates … are allowed."
So a price that depends on post-closing results cannot be structured as an earnout paid to the seller
when the buyer uses a 7(a) loan; the deal's SBA lender confirms the permitted alternatives.
[KB-VSALE-080] **(official, SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, effective 2026-10-01, and SOP 50 10 8, retrieved
2026-09-28)**

### 3.4 Seller staying on after a complete sale — as a CONSULTANT only

**SBA rules affirmatively ban the seller from staying on as an employee.** Under SOP 50 10 8.1 (and
50 10 8 before it), "the seller in a Business Expansion or Initial Acquisition change of ownership
transaction may not remain as an officer, director, stockholder, or employee of the business." If a
transitional period is needed, "the small business may contract with the seller as a consultant" —
under SOP 50 10 8 that consulting period was capped at 12 months; **SOP 50 10 8.1 raises the cap to
24 months, in aggregate, including any extensions.** (This ban applies to a complete (100%) change of ownership. Two exceptions: in a **partial**
change of ownership, where the seller keeps part of the business, the seller may stay as an owner,
officer or employee and is subject to the guarantee rules in §3.3; and ESOP-purchaser
deals, where the seller may remain as an owner, officer, director, stockholder, or employee under
specific conditions the SOP defines separately — not the ordinary arm's-length sale this file is
about.) **This is a hard program rule, not a negotiated deal term: "employee" is not an available
post-closing role for the seller in an SBA-financed deal, only "consultant," and only for up to 24
months in aggregate.** **(official, SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, effective 2026-10-01, and SOP 50 10 8, both
read directly from the .docx published at legacy.sba.gov, retrieved 2026-09-28 — third-party sources
including Live Oak Bank and Reins describe the seller staying on "as a paid employee or consultant,"
which is not accurate for an SBA-financed deal)** [KB-VSALE-027]

**This directly affects §4's licensed-qualifier bridge option.** If the deal needs the seller to stay
on as the licensed qualifier to bridge a licensing gap (§4), that arrangement must be structured as a
consulting relationship capped at 24 months, never as employment — confirm with the deal's SBA lender
and a state-licensing attorney whether a 24-month consulting cap is workable given the buyer's actual
timeline to obtain their own license, since a licensing process that runs longer than 24 months
cannot be bridged by keeping the seller on past that cap in an SBA-financed deal.

The length and terms of any consulting period are still a negotiated deal term within the 24-month
ceiling, not itself an SBA-mandated length; a longer negotiated transition (up to the cap) is a signal
the buyer (or their lender) has concerns about owner-dependence risk (see §2.1) that the deal price or
structure has not fully priced in. **This paragraph describes the general SBA framework reported
above — the specific terms of any consulting arrangement, and its tax treatment, should be reviewed
by the seller's own attorney and CPA before signing anything, and confirmed with the deal's SBA
lender.**

## 4. License and insurance transfer for contractors — general principles

**One state's board was checked for this file — California's — and its rule is that a contractor's
license belongs to the license-holder (an individual or, in some states, a qualifying individual tied
to a specific business entity) and does NOT transfer automatically to a new owner on sale of the
business,** even when the business name, phone number, and address all stay the same, and that
**licenses are associated with a business entity, not necessarily the qualifier.** California's
Contractors State License Board (CSLB) states plainly: "licenses are not transferable from one
business to another, even if the qualifying individual is the same for both," and when a business is
sold, "the license number cannot be transferred to the new company" absent a specific, narrow
exception (official, CSLB "Change in Business Entity," retrieved 2026-09-28). **No other state's
board was checked for this file — the "generally does NOT transfer automatically" framing above is
this file's own generalization from the single California example, not a claim that every state's
board works the same way; confirm the actual rule for any other state directly with that state's
board or counsel.** [KB-VSALE-028]

**CSLB's narrow exceptions** (illustrative of the kind of exception that may exist in other states,
NOT a claim that other states mirror these specific exceptions): a sole-owner license may pass to an
immediate family member only if the original licensee is deceased or absent; a sole-owner license
may be reassigned to a corporation the licensee formed, only if the licensee retains at least 51%
ownership of that new corporation (and once reassigned this way, it can never be changed back to a
sole-owner license); and certain corporate-structure changes (mergers, subsidiary formation) may
carry a license forward when the same underlying entity continues (official, CSLB "Change in
Business Entity," retrieved 2026-09-28). None of these exceptions covers an arm's-length sale of the
business to an unrelated buyer. [KB-VSALE-029]

**What this means practically for a sale:** in the ordinary case of selling to an unrelated buyer,
the buyer (or an employee the buyer designates, or the buyer personally) will need to hold, or newly
obtain, their own qualifying license before the business can legally operate under new ownership.
Obtaining an original license typically requires passing trade and law/business exams, meeting
experience requirements, and posting a new license bond — none of which is instant. **This is the
single most important reason a documented transition plan matters in a services-business sale**: if
the buyer cannot become licensed (or does not already hold an equivalent license) before closing,
the deal needs either (a) the seller staying on long enough as the licensed qualifier to bridge the
gap (structured as a **consulting** arrangement capped at 24 months in an SBA-financed deal — never
as employment, and never as an ownership stake — see §3.4), or (b)
the buyer lining up a licensed employee or partner before closing, or (c) a closing delay while the
buyer tests for and obtains their own license. [KB-VSALE-030]

**State-specific rules vary and must be confirmed with counsel or the specific state licensing
board before structuring a deal.** This file states CSLB's rule as a worked, sourced example of the
general shape of the problem (licenses are personal/entity-bound, not freely transferable, and
change-of-ownership triggers a notification or re-application requirement, commonly within a bounded
window such as CSLB's 90-day notification rule) — it is not a claim that every state's contractor
licensing board works identically. **Bring the seller's actual license type, the target state's
licensing statute, and the proposed deal structure (asset sale vs. entity/stock sale — see below) to
a licensed attorney in that state before finalizing deal terms that depend on license continuity.**
[KB-VSALE-031]

**Asset sale vs. entity sale matters directly here.** An asset-sale structure (the buyer buys the
business's assets, contracts, and goodwill, and a new or existing buyer entity operates going
forward) squarely triggers the "new company, needs its own license" problem described above. An
entity/stock sale (the buyer buys the ownership interests of the existing licensed entity itself,
leaving the same legal entity in place) keeps the same entity that holds the license — since CSLB's
own rule is that a license is associated with a business entity, not necessarily the qualifier, a
stock purchase of a licensed corporation does not by itself trigger the "new company needs its own
license" problem the way an asset sale does. **This does not mean a stock sale is automatically
license-continuity-safe:** CSLB still requires notification (and, in some cases, re-application) on
certain changes to a licensed entity — its "Change in Business Entity" process governs exactly this —
so a change in ownership of the entity can still trigger a CSLB filing requirement even though the
entity itself continues. Whether a given state treats a stock sale differently from an asset sale for
license-continuity purposes, and exactly what filing a stock sale triggers, is a state-specific legal
question, not a portable rule — confirm it with counsel before choosing a deal
structure on the assumption that a stock sale sidesteps the license problem. [KB-VSALE-032]

### 4.1 Insurance transfer

General liability and workers' compensation insurance policies are contracts between the insurer and
the named insured business entity — like licenses, they do not automatically transfer to a new owner
in an asset sale, and a new entity will need to bind its own coverage before operating. In an entity
sale, the existing policies may be able to continue under the same named insured, but most carriers
require notification of a change in ownership/control and may re-underwrite the account at that
point (insurers commonly treat a change of ownership as a material change in risk). A lapse in
coverage during a transition — even briefly — is both an operational risk (an uninsured loss on an
open job) and a diligence red flag a sophisticated buyer will specifically check for (see §5). A
seller with any history of a lapsed license or lapsed insurance should expect a buyer to ask about it
directly and should have a straight, documented explanation ready rather than letting a buyer
discover it independently. [KB-VSALE-033]

## 5. What a buyer diligences

A buyer of a local service business — whether an individual SBA-financed buyer or a small strategic
acquirer — will typically verify, at minimum:

- **Financial verification**: three years of business tax returns reconciled against the internal
  P&L/bookkeeping, bank statements matched to deposits, and the SDE add-back schedule tested
  line-by-line against receipts (an add-back a seller cannot document with a receipt or a clear
  business rationale should be expected to be challenged or removed). [KB-VSALE-034]
- **Customer concentration and contract review**: revenue by customer/referral-source over the
  trailing 12–36 months, to test the concentration risk described in §2.1; any written contracts,
  maintenance agreements, or referral arrangements, and whether they are assignable to a new owner
  or require the counterparty's consent to assign (many commercial contracts and some insurance
  carrier "preferred vendor" agreements are not freely assignable — this must be checked, not
  assumed). [KB-VSALE-035]
- **License and insurance status**: current license standing with the state board, license history
  (any suspensions, complaints, or lapses), current certificates of insurance, and loss-run reports
  from the insurer showing claims history — a claims history is not automatically disqualifying but
  will be priced into the deal or the buyer's post-closing insurance cost. [KB-VSALE-036]
- **Open jobs and warranty exposure**: every job in progress at closing, its contract terms, deposits
  already collected versus work remaining, and any outstanding warranty obligations on completed
  work — these are typically negotiated as either retained by the seller (with an escrow or holdback
  to cover them) or assumed by the buyer with a corresponding price adjustment. [KB-VSALE-037]
- **Employee and subcontractor dependencies**: which employees or subs are critical to delivery, and
  whether any hold licenses or certifications the business depends on (see §4), and whether key
  employees are likely to stay through and after the transition. [KB-VSALE-038]
- **Litigation and liens**: any pending litigation, mechanic's liens filed against the business's
  jobs, or unresolved customer disputes. [KB-VSALE-039]
- **Lead source and marketing dependency**: how leads currently arrive (see §6) — a buyer will ask
  whether lead flow is systematized (paid channels with known cost-per-lead, a website, referral
  program) or entirely dependent on the owner's personal reputation and relationships, because the
  latter does not transfer. [KB-VSALE-040]
- **Related-party transactions**: any revenue or expense line involving a relationship the seller
  controls (see §2.1's related-party revenue point) will be specifically probed and typically
  adjusted out of SDE if it does not represent an arm's-length, ongoing market relationship.
  [KB-VSALE-041]

## 6. Rebuilding the marketing and lead engine before a sale

A local service business's revenue after closing depends entirely on whether new customers keep
finding it — and for many owner-run trade businesses, the honest answer at any given moment is "the
owner's personal network and reputation," which is exactly the owner-dependence risk factor from
§2.1 that depresses the multiple. Rebuilding a documented, transferable lead engine before going to
market is one of the highest-leverage things a seller can do to raise the sale price, because it
converts an unpriceable risk (will leads keep coming after the owner leaves) into a priceable,
diligence-able asset (a known, working system with real numbers behind it). [KB-VSALE-042]

**What "documented lead flow" means in practice, and why a buyer will pay for it:**

- **A tracked, attributable lead source mix** — knowing, for a meaningful trailing period, what share
  of leads came from paid search, paid social, organic/local search, referrals, and repeat customers,
  with real numbers rather than an owner's impression. [KB-VSALE-043]
- **A known cost per lead and cost per booked job by channel** — if the business spends a
  documented $X to generate a lead through a given channel, and a documented conversion rate from
  lead to booked job, a buyer can underwrite future revenue instead of guessing at it. This is the
  single most concrete way to de-risk the "where does next month's revenue come from" question a
  buyer and their lender will ask. [KB-VSALE-044]
- **Booked-job economics** — average job value, gross margin by job type, and the sales cycle length
  from lead to signed contract, documented well enough that a new owner (or their hired
  sales/estimating hire) could step into the process using the seller's own numbers rather than
  starting from zero. [KB-VSALE-045]
- **A functioning system, not just historical numbers** — a live website generating organic leads, an
  active paid-channel account (even a modest one) with real performance history, and a CRM or
  equivalent record of leads and their outcomes, all of which the buyer can inspect during diligence
  and literally take over operating on day one. [KB-VSALE-046]

**Timing matters.** These systems need real operating history to be credible in diligence — a lead
engine turned on the month before listing the business for sale will read to a sophisticated buyer
as exactly that, and will not carry the same weight as one with 12+ months of consistent, documented
performance. A seller planning a sale in the next 12–24 months should treat building this evidence
base as part of the sale-preparation work, not something to assemble after a buyer asks
(internal doctrine, retrieved 2026-09-28 — the general logic connecting evidence maturity to
diligence credibility; no specific minimum-months figure is sourced to any outside authority, so
treat any specific month count as a planning heuristic, not a rule). [KB-VSALE-047]

## Decision rules

- **If** the deal is expected to price under ~$2M **then** anchor valuation conversations to SDE
  multiples (§1.1), not EBITDA, and confirm which base any comparable deal or broker quote is using
  before comparing it. [KB-VSALE-002], [KB-VSALE-006]
- **If** the owner personally holds the only trade license in the business, has no second-in-command
  who can sell or run a job independently, and has concentrated customer relationships **then**
  expect the multiple to price at or below the bottom of the size band's range, and expect a buyer
  or lender to require a longer post-closing transition period (§2.1, §3.4). Treat fixing these
  before going to market as higher-leverage than almost anything else available to the seller.
- **If** the seller wants a completely clean exit with no ongoing SBA personal-guarantee obligation
  **then** structure the sale as 100% of ownership transferring at closing — confirm with the deal's
  SBA lender and attorney/CPA before agreeing, since retaining under 20% still triggers a full-loan
  guarantee for at least two years and retaining 20% or more triggers a full-loan-term guarantee
  (§3.3). A seller wishing to stay involved should do so as a **consultant** (capped at 24 months in
  aggregate); in a complete sale, SBA rules ban the seller from staying as an employee, officer,
  director or stockholder (§3.4). A seller who keeps part of the business (partial change of
  ownership) may stay in those roles, under the §3.3 guarantee rules.
- **If** a buyer's deal structure leans on a seller note to satisfy the SBA equity-injection
  requirement **then** confirm with the deal's SBA lender and attorney/CPA before agreeing: the
  seller should expect zero payments on that portion of the note while it remains on full standby, but
  under SOP 50 10 8.1 that seller debt becomes eligible to be refinanced once it has been in place and
  current for 36 months — model post-closing cash needs against that 36-month point, not blindly
  against the full loan term (commonly 10 years) (§3.2).
- **If** the deal is structured as an asset sale (the common case for a sale to an unrelated buyer)
  **then** confirm with the deal's SBA lender and attorney/CPA before agreeing: assume the buyer will
  need their own license and their own insurance binder before closing or very shortly after, and
  build the closing timeline and any seller-transition period around that reality — do not assume the
  seller's license or policies simply carry over (§4, §4.1).
- **If** the seller has any license lapse, insurance lapse, or claims history **then** disclose it
  proactively with a clear, documented explanation before a buyer's diligence finds it independently
  — a disclosed and explained gap reads very differently from a discovered one (§4.1, §5).
- **If** the business has no documented lead-generation system with real performance history **and**
  a sale is planned within the next 12–24 months **then** prioritize building one (tracked channel
  mix, cost per lead, booked-job economics) well before listing, because a system with real operating
  history is materially more credible in diligence than one assembled just before a sale (§6).
- **If** any question in this file turns on a specific state's licensing statute, a specific
  insurance policy's assignability, or a specific loan's underwriting terms **then** route it to a
  licensed attorney, CPA, or the deal's own SBA lender — this file gives general principles and
  sourced ranges only, never a specific legal or lending determination for one company's actual
  facts.

## Anti-patterns

- **Quoting a multiple from memory or from a single skimmed headline** ("service businesses sell for
  3x") without naming which survey, which price band, and which base (SDE vs. EBITDA) it came from.
  Always cite the specific survey and band (§1.1). [KB-VSALE-003], [KB-VSALE-004]
- **Counting related-party or one-off revenue at face value in SDE** and presenting the resulting
  multiple as if it applies to the real, ongoing business — a buyer's diligence will strip this out,
  and presenting an inflated SDE to a buyer or lender is also a disclosure problem, not just a
  valuation error (§2.1, §5). [KB-VSALE-013]
- **Assuming a stock/entity sale automatically preserves the contractor license** without checking
  the specific state's rule — some states' license-continuity rules key off ownership change even in
  an entity sale (§4). [KB-VSALE-032]
- **Treating an SBA seller note as near-term cash for the full loan term** in the seller's own
  financial planning — if it is structured to satisfy the equity-injection requirement, it is on full
  standby with zero payments, and it can only be paid off early through a new 7(a) loan's proceeds once it has been in place
  and current for 36 months (SOP 50 10 8.1), which requires a lender to underwrite that refinancing;
  otherwise it stays on standby for the life of the original loan (§3.2). [KB-VSALE-024]
- **Assuming any retained ownership stake gets the same treatment.** A first-time seller may not
  realize that retaining under 20% triggers a full-loan-amount guarantee for at least two years, while
  retaining 20% or more triggers the standard full-loan-term guarantee that applies to any 20%+ owner
  — these are not the same obligation, and "even 1% is the same as 40%" is not correct (§3.3).
  [KB-VSALE-026]
- **Describing the seller's post-closing role as "employee or consultant."** SBA rules ban the seller
  from remaining an officer, director, stockholder, or employee outright; only a consulting
  arrangement, capped at 24 months in aggregate under SOP 50 10 8.1, is available (§3.4).
  [KB-VSALE-027]
- **Building the lead-generation story the month before listing** and presenting it as an established
  system — real buyers and their advisors will ask for operating history, and a system with no track
  record reads as a projection, not an asset (§6).
- **Giving a specific legal or lending answer** ("your state definitely allows license transfer in a
  stock sale," "your seller note definitely can be structured with early payments") instead of
  naming the general rule and routing the specific-facts question to counsel, a CPA, or the deal's
  SBA lender, per Rule 5 of this KB's shared writing brief.
