# Co-founder search: channels, process, evaluation

## What this file answers

This file is the working reference for helping a solo, first-time US founder — who already codes
with AI agents and already has a mostly-built product — find and vet a technical co-founder and/or
a business/sales co-founder. It covers where experienced, previously-exited operators actually
spend time looking for their next thing; how to structure a first call; how to run a real trial
project instead of a leap of faith; a scorecard and decision rules for go/no-go; how to run a
back-channel reference check; a red-flags checklist; realistic search timelines from published
data; and how to pitch a mostly-built, unfunded product honestly without overselling it. Venture
department agents (`venture-director`, `capital-and-exit-strategist`, `bizdev-outbound-strategist`,
`partner-recruiter`) should answer from this file rather than from memory. Equity math, vesting
mechanics, and cliff/vesting-schedule numbers live in the companion file
`equity-and-agreements.md` — this file points to it rather than duplicating it. Nothing here is
legal advice; anywhere paperwork or classification is at stake, the guidance is to bring a real
employment/startup attorney into the loop, not to draft or sign anything based on this file alone.

## 1. Where experienced and exited founders actually congregate

The single biggest mistake a first-time founder makes in co-founder search is treating it like a
job posting — one channel, one post, then waiting. Experienced operators are spread across a
handful of channels, each with different mechanics, different populations, and different platform
rules. Work more than one at once.

### YC Co-Founder Matching

**What it is.** A free platform, run by Y Combinator but open to anyone — YC program affiliation
or acceptance is explicitly not required, and using it neither helps nor hurts a YC application
`[KB-VREC-001]` (official, ycombinator.com/cofounder-matching, retrieved 2026-09-28). It takes no
equity and has "no strings attached" `[KB-VREC-002]` (official, ycombinator.com/cofounder-matching,
retrieved 2026-09-28).

**Mechanics.** Sign-up runs through Startup School (startupschool.org) `[KB-VREC-003]` (official,
ycombinator.com/cofounder-matching, retrieved 2026-09-28). The flow is: (1) create a profile
describing yourself, your stage, your skills, and what you're looking for; (2) the matching engine
surfaces profiles that fit your stated preferences; (3) you send an invite to connect to anyone
who looks promising; (4) if they accept, that's a match, and you schedule a real conversation from
there `[KB-VREC-004]` (official, ycombinator.com/cofounder-matching, retrieved 2026-09-28).
Profiles are private — visible only to other approved co-founder-matching users, not indexed
publicly — specifically so a person can search while still employed without their current employer
finding out `[KB-VREC-005]` (official, ycombinator.com/cofounder-matching, retrieved 2026-09-28).

**Who's on it.** The platform accepts people at any stage — already building full-time, or just
thinking about it, with or without an idea yet `[KB-VREC-006]` (official,
ycombinator.com/cofounder-matching, retrieved 2026-09-28). Scale is real: 150,000+ profiles as of
2025 and over 100,000 matches made across the network to date, with thousands of active profiles
concentrated in SF and NYC `[KB-VREC-007]` (third-party, ycinsight.com "YC Co-Founder Matching:
Find the Right Cofounder," retrieved 2026-09-28 — an independent guide site, not YC's own
publication; directional, re-verify count before quoting precisely). Several YC-funded companies
(cited by name in YC's own materials: Whalesync, AccessOwl, Pledge Health, clearspace) trace their
founding team directly to a co-founder-matching connection `[KB-VREC-008]` (official,
ycombinator.com/cofounder-matching, retrieved 2026-09-28).

**Realistic expectation.** The single highest-density, highest-trust pool of serious,
currently-searching founders in the US, and free — use as a primary channel, not a backup. Skews
technical/early-career more than already-exited operators; treat acquirer-alumni and operator-angel
channels below as the better source for someone who has already built and sold a company.

**Profile-writing guidance.** Lead with what is real, not aspirational: current stage (mostly-built
product, X users/revenue if any), what you need (a specific skill gap — "technical co-founder who
can own the backend and DevOps," not "someone technical"), and what you bring (existing product,
any revenue, domain expertise). Vague profiles ("passionate about building something great
together") get skipped; specific ones get responses.

### Wellfound (formerly AngelList Talent)

**What it is.** A startup jobs and recruiting platform with 5 million+ registered users, many of
whom are open to a co-founder role rather than pure employment if the equity is meaningful
`[KB-VREC-009]` (third-party, aggregated from search of Wellfound's own site content, retrieved
2026-09-28). Wellfound is built primarily as an employer/candidate job board, not a co-founder-only
platform, so a co-founder search there requires explicitly signaling that in the role title and
description — a generic "software engineer" posting will attract job-seekers, not partnership
candidates `[KB-VREC-010]` (third-party, aggregated site-content search, retrieved 2026-09-28).

**Mechanics.** Companies (or individuals) run searches through a sourcing/filter tool — role
category, location, keyword, and a "diversity boost" filter — and message candidates directly
rather than waiting for inbound applications; Wellfound's own help documentation confirms the
search/filter mechanics but this pass could not confirm the specific messaging-limit or paid-tier
line-up from Wellfound's own pricing page — UNVERIFIED (primary source unreachable:
https://wellfound.com/pricing) `[KB-VREC-011]`. Before relying on a specific messaging quota or
paid-plan price, re-fetch Wellfound's live pricing page or a current account.

**Realistic expectation.** Good for reaching individual engineers/operators who are job-searching
but open to equity-heavy co-founder roles, especially outside the SF/YC bubble. Weaker than YC
matching or founder communities for reaching people who are already deliberately co-founder-hunting
(most Wellfound traffic is job-seeking, not co-founder-seeking) — post there as a supplementary
channel, and screen hard for "wants employee-with-upside" vs. "wants true co-founder" (see the red
flags section).

### Founder communities

**On Deck (ODF — On Deck Founders).** A fellowship centered on a one-week in-person cohort (the
program's own site, as of this retrieval, names "ODF28" kicking off Q1 2027 — cohort numbering and
dates change; re-verify the current/next cohort directly before quoting one) plus lifelong access to
an alumni community of 3,000+ members used for co-founder matching, mentorship, and peer support
`[KB-VREC-012]` (third-party, aggregated from joinodf.com and elev-x.com program-review content,
retrieved 2026-09-28). It is explicitly non-dilutive — the program takes no equity
`[KB-VREC-013]` (official, joinodf.com, retrieved 2026-09-28, verbatim: "Non-dilutive... We don't
take any equity"). **The specific "pay what you can... ~$1,000" pricing and the "over half of ODF
companies met their co-founder through the program" statistic could not be confirmed on joinodf.com
as fetched this pass** `[KB-VREC-014]` UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation (joinodf.com's current
content shows individual companies marked as having met co-founders via ODF, but states no
percentage, and states no specific contribution amount; both figures may have appeared in an older
version of the site or in a secondary aggregator and should be re-confirmed directly against
joinodf.com, or dropped, before being repeated to a founder). Note participation is a real cost and
time commitment (an in-person week), unlike the free platforms above — worth it primarily if the
founder wants a curated, vetted peer pool rather than an open marketplace.

**Indie Hackers.** A large, free, asynchronous community of solo and small-team founders (many
bootstrapped, many post-exit or already profitable) organized around forums and product
show-and-tell. No official co-founder-matching feature comparable to YC's, but active
"looking for a co-founder" threads and a real population of experienced operators who have already
built and shipped something. Free, no application gate — lower signal density than YC matching or
ODF, but zero cost and zero friction to try.

**First Round community / First Round Review.** Not an open membership (First Round is a VC firm;
in-network events generally require a portfolio-company connection), but First Round Review — their
public blog — is one of the most substantive free published resources on co-founder search process.
See §2 and §3 for content pulled from it directly.

**YC's own alumni network (Bookface).** Restricted to founders who have gone through a YC batch —
not usable by a first-time founder without one. YC Co-Founder Matching (above) is the open-access
alternative YC itself points non-alumni toward.

**Industry-specific Slack/Discord communities.** Vertical-specific communities vary widely in
quality and moderation. Useful for reaching a business/sales co-founder with actual domain
expertise — a channel a technical-only search misses. Vet each community's own posting rules before
soliciting (some forbid recruiting pitches outright, others have a dedicated channel for it).

### Operator/angel networks

**Alumni syndicates on AngelList.** AngelList hosts named alumni syndicates (e.g., a well-known
company's own early-employee/alumni network) where current and former employees of a specific
successful company act as an organized group of angel investors and operators who mentor and
introduce founders `[KB-VREC-015]` (third-party, angellist.com "Alumni Syndicates" page content via
search aggregation, retrieved 2026-09-28 — re-fetch angellist.com/alumni-syndicates directly before
citing a specific syndicate name or count). These are a strong pool for finding an experienced,
domain-relevant operator, because the people running and participating in them have already been
through a successful exit and often want to advise or co-found something new. Access is generally
through introduction rather than open application — a warm intro from any existing connection at
the underlying company is the practical way in.

**Syndicate leads themselves.** The people who run AngelList syndicates are frequently former
founders, technical operators, or early hires at successful companies who are well-connected and
good at recognizing promising teams even without large amounts of their own capital to invest
`[KB-VREC-016]` (third-party, aggregated AngelList/outlander.vc content on syndicate structure,
retrieved 2026-09-28). A syndicate lead who likes a founder's pitch is often willing to make direct
introductions to potential co-founders in their network, even without investing themselves at this
stage.

**Realistic expectation.** This channel runs on warm introductions, not open applications — it is
slower to enter than YC matching or Wellfound but produces a higher floor on candidate quality
(people who have already built and often exited something real) once you're in it.

### Acquirer alumni

**Why this is a strong pool.** Someone who sold a company to a strategic acquirer has already
proven they can build something a buyer wanted, has usually just come off a retention/earnout
period with liquidity and free time, and — if the acquisition was in an adjacent space — brings
real domain relevance to a new product in a related vertical. This population self-selects for
exactly the profile a first-time founder wants in a co-founder: proven builder, proven finisher
(they got to a real exit, not just a launch), and financially able to take co-founder-level risk
again.

**How to reach them.** There is no single platform indexing "recently exited founders looking for
their next thing" — this pool is found through: (a) the acquirer's own alumni/portfolio pages and
press releases naming the acquired team, (b) LinkedIn searches for "co-founder" + "[acquiring
company] (acquired)" in a person's work history, (c) accelerator/incubator alumni directories
(Founders Network, for instance, explicitly builds a support network from accelerator alumni who
have gone on to raise Series A and exit) `[KB-VREC-017]` (third-party, foundersnetwork.com,
retrieved 2026-09-28), and (d) direct warm introduction through the operator-angel networks above,
since many syndicate members are exactly this population. There is no dedicated public
"acquihire alumni" directory as of this writing — treat this as a targeted-outreach channel
(`bizdev-outbound-strategist`'s lane), not a browse-and-apply platform.

### Local ecosystem

**Startup meetups, university entrepreneurship centers, local accelerators, co-working spaces.**
Lower-tech, higher-touch, and underrated. A university entrepreneurship center (even one the
founder didn't attend — most host public events) and a local accelerator's demo nights or open
office hours put a founder in a room self-selected for wanting to build something, often including
advisors, mentors, and alumni looking for their next project. Startup-focused co-working spaces
concentrate the same population and usually run a bulletin board or Slack for "looking for a
co-founder" posts. No platform mechanics here — the value is in showing up repeatedly, not one post.

venue's own stated rules on recruiting pitches and off-platform contact — some restrict cold
recruiting to designated channels, and violating that norm burns trust fast in a small ecosystem.
This file cannot enumerate every community's current rules; check them live before posting.

## 2. First-call structure (30-45 minutes)

A first call with a candidate co-founder is not a sales pitch and not a job interview — it's a
mutual screen. Order matters: build rapport and establish real interest before asking hard
questions, but do not let the whole call pass without surfacing at least one potentially
disqualifying topic.

**Suggested order:**

1. **Warm open (3-5 min).** How you found them / what drew you to reach out. Let them talk about
   what they're looking for before you pitch anything — you learn more this way than by leading
   with your own story.
2. **Your story, honestly (5-8 min).** What the product is, what stage it's really at (see §8 for
   how to be honest about this), why you're looking for a co-founder now rather than hiring an
   employee. Name the specific gap you need filled — do not describe an abstract "great co-founder"
   wishlist.
3. **Their story (8-10 min).** Background, what they've built before, why they're looking now, what
   they want out of a next venture (equity-driven ownership vs. steady income vs. mission-driven).
   Listen for whether their stated motivation matches what a true co-founder role actually offers —
   this is where the "wants employee benefits with founder title" mismatch often first surfaces (see
   red flags, §6).
4. **Working style and values (8-10 min).** How they handle conflict, how they made a hard call at a
   past company, what a bad day working together would look like. First Round's own guidance is to
   probe specifically for how someone handles conflict and resolves disagreement, since this is the
   single highest-leverage predictor of whether a partnership survives real stress
   `[KB-VREC-018]` (third-party, First Round Review "Looking for Love in All The Wrong Places,"
   retrieved 2026-09-28).
5. **Logistics (3-5 min).** Location/timezone, current commitments (day job, other projects, family
   constraints), runway (can they go without salary, and for how long), rough equity expectations
   (do not negotiate a number on the first call — just confirm you're both in a plausible range; see
   `equity-and-agreements.md` for the inputs to the late-joiner conversation — there is no fixed range).
6. **Close with a concrete next step (2-3 min).** Never end a promising call with "let's stay in
   touch" — propose a specific next action: a second call, a shared small project, or an intro to
   see the product. Vague warmth without a next step is how promising conversations quietly die.

**Questions worth asking directly (not exhaustive — see the 50-question resource below for a
fuller list to use across multiple conversations, not in one sitting):**
- "Walk me through a time you disagreed with a business partner about something important. What
  happened?"
- "What does 'done' look like for you on this — are you looking to build for 10 years, or to build
  and exit in 2-3?"
- "What's your runway — how long can you go without a salary from this?"
- "What would make you walk away from a co-founder relationship?"

**Red flags to listen for on the first call:**
- Cannot describe a single specific disagreement or hard call from past work — either inexperienced
  at partnership or unwilling to show vulnerability.
- Talks only about title, equity percentage, or "founder" as a label, never about the actual work.
- Vague or evasive about current commitments (won't say whether they have a day job, other
  projects, or how much runway they have).
- Wants to skip straight to "let's just start building" with no discussion of roles, equity, or
  working agreement — this is excitement, not diligence, and it is a pattern that tends to produce
  unresolved resentment later, not a good sign of speed.

**How to describe the opportunity honestly.** State plainly: what's built, what isn't, whether there
is revenue, whether there are existing investors or advisors with a claim on the cap table (see §8),
and what the specific role gap is. A first-time founder's instinct is to oversell to make the
opportunity sound more attractive — resist this. An experienced candidate will find the gaps within
a few real conversations regardless, and finding them out from you first builds trust; finding them
out later reads as concealment.

## 3. Trial projects — "dating before marriage"

Every credible source on co-founder search converges on the same core advice: never commit to a
co-founder relationship (and never sign an equity split or vesting agreement) without a real,
bounded trial working together first.

### Paid vs. unpaid — the legal issue, briefly

Whether a trial collaborator — or any founder — can legally work unpaid is a genuine legal risk
area, not a matter of convenience or informal agreement between the parties. **There is no blanket
rule that a co-founder can go unpaid just by being called a co-founder.** A founder who works for
the company can still be an employee under wage law; the federal exemption covers only an owner of
at least a bona-fide 20% equity interest who is also actively engaged in management (29 CFR
541.101), and several states — California among them — have no equivalent exemption at all
`[KB-VREC-019]` (third-party, aggregated legal-blog content — Westaway, Bend Law Group, San Jose
Business Lawyers Blog — via search, retrieved 2026-09-28; the 20%-plus-active-management federal
threshold itself is `(official, 29 CFR 541.101, retrieved 2026-09-28)`; treat the blog aggregation
as directional legal-landscape information, not a substitute for counsel). Someone doing trial work
who holds no real equity at all is even more clearly at risk of being legally an employee, and under
the federal Fair Labor Standards Act an employee must be paid at least minimum wage and overtime
unless a specific exemption applies. Whether any specific founder or co-founder can lawfully go
unpaid is an employment-attorney decision — never assumed from a "co-founder" title alone.
Misclassifying a trial collaborator as an
unpaid-owner when they are legally an employee creates real exposure: back wages, back payroll
taxes (state, federal, FICA) with interest, and — notably — a displaced or soured trial
collaborator later filing a wage claim is exactly the kind of dispute that can also spook a future
investor during diligence `[KB-VREC-020]` (third-party, same aggregation, retrieved 2026-09-28).
**This file does not resolve how to structure a specific trial-work arrangement — that analysis,
including when an unpaid arrangement is actually defensible (e.g., a genuine, from-day-one equity
co-founder with a real ownership stake vs. someone being trialed for a role) belongs in
`equity-and-agreements.md`, and any real arrangement should be reviewed by an employment attorney
before it starts, not after a dispute arises.** The point for this file is narrower: know before
you start a trial that "we'll pay in equity" is not automatically a safe legal structure, and
build in the time to get that checked rather than assuming a handshake covers it.

### Scoping a real trial

**Length.** The most commonly cited range across sources is **two to four weeks** for a single
trial project `[KB-VREC-021]` (third-party, aggregated from ycombinator.com library content via
search and First Round Review's "founder dating" case study, retrieved 2026-09-28). One detailed
public account (Gloria Lin, First Round Review) describes "dating" six different potential
co-founders over the course of a year, then spending **four months specifically working with her
eventual co-founder** to validate the fit before formally committing `[KB-VREC-022]` (third-party,
First Round Review "The Founder Dating Playbook," retrieved 2026-09-28, verbatim: "I ended up
'dating' six different potential co-founders over the course of a year" and "I spent four months
working with my current co-founder to validate that we were on the right track"). Read these as two
different things: the **overall search**, across multiple candidates, ran about a year; the **final
working trial with the person she actually chose** was four months — "four months total" describes
the last stage, not the whole search (see §7 for the full realistic-timeline breakdown, which this
figure must stay consistent with).

**How to scope it so it's a real test, not theater:**
- Pick a task that requires the actual skill you're evaluating — for a technical candidate, a real
  scoped feature or bug fix in the actual (or a representative) codebase, not a generic take-home
  exercise; for a business/sales candidate, real outreach to real prospects (with the founder's
  knowledge and within any platform/compliance rules) or a real pricing/positioning analysis against
  the actual product, not a hypothetical case study.
- Agree upfront on: the specific deliverable, the deadline, and — critically — **who owns the work
  product at the end**, especially if the candidate does not become a co-founder. **A signed CIIAA
  (attorney-provided or the company's attorney-drafted equivalent) must exist before the trial
  starts — an email restating the agreement is not a substitute** (`equity-and-agreements.md` §8
  requires a signed CIIAA before any code, design, or IP contribution, with no exception for trial
  work). An email can usefully restate the deliverable and deadline for clarity, but ownership itself
  is settled by the CIIAA, not by informal correspondence.
- Decide upfront what you're actually measuring: not just "was the output good" but how they
  communicated blockers, whether they asked good clarifying questions, how they reacted to
  feedback or a change in scope mid-trial, and whether working together felt like it made the
  founder's own load lighter or heavier.
- Consumer product ideas lend themselves to a lightweight built artifact (a working prototype, a
  landing page, a small feature); enterprise/B2B ideas often lend themselves better to real customer
  discovery calls as the trial task, since the skill being tested (can this person credibly talk to
  a prospective customer) doesn't require code at all `[KB-VREC-023]` (third-party, First Round
  Review "The Founder Dating Playbook," retrieved 2026-09-28).

**What to measure, concretely:** deliverable quality against the agreed scope; communication
frequency and clarity during the trial (not just at the end); how they handled at least one
moment of ambiguity or disagreement during the trial itself; whether they finished what they said
they would, on the timeline they proposed.

## 4. Scorecards and decision rules

### Dimensions to score

Score each serious candidate, ideally after both a real conversation and a completed trial project,
across these dimensions:

1. **Hard skills fit.** Do they actually have the specific skill you lack (not an adjacent skill)?
   For a technical co-founder: can they demonstrably ship the kind of thing your product still
   needs. For a business/sales co-founder: can they demonstrably sell or grow something comparable
   in complexity/price point to what you're selling.
2. **Complementary vs. redundant skillset.** Two technical people (or two sales people) with no one
   covering the other half of the business is a common early mistake — score explicitly whether
   this candidate fills a gap you don't already cover, not whether they're impressive in the
   abstract.
3. **Values alignment.** Do your answers to "what does success look like in 3 years" and "how much
   risk are you willing to take" actually match, not just sound compatible in the moment.
4. **Conflict-handling.** Based on the specific story they told in §2's conflict question and — if
   the trial produced any real disagreement — how they actually behaved when it happened, not how
   they say they would behave.
5. **Commitment level / runway.** Can they actually go unpaid or underpaid for the realistic
   timeline to revenue or funding, given their real financial situation (ask directly — a candidate
   who is financially unable to take founder-level risk right now is not being dishonest by saying
   so, but it does mean a true equal-equity co-founder role is not yet the right fit for them; an
   advisor or paid-contractor-first arrangement may be).
6. **Geography/timezone fit.** Can you actually work together in real time often enough for the
   working style you need (see §edge cases below for what to do when the best candidate is in a
   different country).

### Plain if/then decision framework

- **IF** the candidate scores strong on hard skills and complementary fit but you have not yet run
  a real trial project together, **THEN** do not finalize equity or title — schedule the trial
  first. A great first call is necessary but never sufficient.
- **IF** the trial project reveals a genuine skill or reliability gap (missed the agreed deadline
  without early communication, delivered materially below the agreed scope), **THEN** treat this as
  a real signal, not bad luck — a first trial is the best-case version of how someone shows up,
  since they're still trying to impress you.
- **IF** the candidate is strong on skills but weak or evasive on values alignment or
  conflict-handling, **THEN** slow down regardless of the skills fit — a skills gap can be hired
  around later; a values or conflict-handling mismatch generally cannot, and is the single most
  commonly cited reason co-founder relationships fail once real stress hits.
  `[KB-VREC-024]` (internal doctrine, retrieved 2026-09-28 — synthesized from the convergent
  emphasis across YC's, First Round's, and legal/HR sources cited throughout this file on
  conflict-handling as the dominant predictor of partnership survival).
- **IF** the candidate refuses to discuss equity, vesting, or a written founder agreement at all
  before or during the trial, **THEN** treat this as a red flag on its own (see §6) and do not
  proceed to a formal offer until it is resolved in conversation.
- **IF** two or more strong candidates are being evaluated at once, **THEN** run comparable trials
  (similar scope and duration) and be transparent with each candidate that you are in parallel
  conversations — concealing this is a trust cost paid later even if it feels efficient now.
- **IF** the only interested candidates want to join as "employee #1 with meaningful options" rather
  than as a true co-founder, **THEN** treat this as real market information, not a failure to hold
  out for — weigh whether an employee-with-strong-equity role (with a real path to more ownership
  tied to milestones) actually solves the underlying need better than continuing to search for a
  title match. Legitimate outcome, not a consolation prize, especially for a product already mostly
  built and de-risked (see §8 on why a later joiner reasonably gets less than a day-one co-founder).
- **IF** only strong technical candidates surface and no credible business/sales candidates (or vice
  versa), **THEN** widen the channel mix toward where the missing skillset actually congregates —
  sales-oriented candidates are found more often via industry communities, local ecosystem events,
  and direct outbound than via YC matching, which skews technical — rather than concluding the
  person doesn't exist.
- **IF** the best candidate lives in a different country, **THEN** separate two questions: (a) can
  you build a working relationship across the timezone gap (be honest about the real-time overlap
  you need, especially early), and (b) what are the legal/tax implications of a foreign co-founder
  holding US equity and working from abroad — genuinely complex (cross-border equity, tax treaties,
  contractor vs. foreign-employee status) and squarely an attorney-and-tax-advisor question, not one
  to resolve with a handshake.

## 5. Reference checks — the back-channel method

A candidate's own supplied references are useful but structurally biased — they were chosen because
they'll say something good. A back-channel reference check means finding people who know the
candidate's actual work but were **not** offered up by the candidate themselves.

**How to find back-channel references.** The two most common sourcing methods: work through the
candidate's LinkedIn connections to identify people who worked directly alongside them, and ask
your own network whether anyone knows this person `[KB-VREC-025]` (third-party, aggregated from
Holloway's "Soliciting Back-Channel References" guide and Bain Capital Ventures' reference-check
field guide, retrieved 2026-09-28). Target people who worked *with* them professionally, not people
who merely know of them socially.

**How to run the conversation.** Best practice: have the person on your own side closest to the
reference make the outreach, in whatever format is natural for that relationship (a text, a
LinkedIn message, a call) `[KB-VREC-026]` (third-party, Holloway's back-channel reference guide,
retrieved 2026-09-28). Frame the ask plainly — "we're considering [name] as a co-founder, and I'd
value anything you can share about what it was like to work with them" — not a leading question.

**What to ask:**
- How closely did you actually work together, and in what context? (Weight the answer accordingly —
  a glowing comment from someone who barely worked with the candidate is worth much less than a
  measured, specific answer from someone who worked closely with them under real pressure.)
- How did they handle a real disagreement or a high-stakes moment?
- Would you work with them again, and why or why not?
- Is there anything you'd want a future co-founder to know that they might not volunteer
  themselves?

**Weighing what you hear.** A reference who is personally close to the candidate may withhold
criticism out of loyalty; a reference who trusts *you* specifically tends to be more candid
`[KB-VREC-027]` (third-party, Holloway back-channel reference guide, retrieved 2026-09-28). Context
and specificity matter more than tone — a specific, mixed account from someone who worked closely
with the candidate is more useful than a uniformly glowing but vague one.

**Red flags in what you hear:** vague praise with no specifics when pressed; any mention of a past
partner or employer who was left in a difficult position (unresolved commitments, a messy exit from
a prior venture) without a credible explanation; a pattern across multiple references of the same
specific concern (e.g., two unconnected references both independently mention the person
struggling to take feedback).

**Ethical boundaries — do this respectfully.** Tell the candidate directly, before you start, that
you may talk to people beyond the references they give you — this is a normal and expected part of
a real reference process, not something to do covertly `[KB-VREC-028]` (third-party, Holloway
back-channel reference guide, retrieved 2026-09-28). Never contact anyone at the candidate's current
employer if they have not told their employer they're exploring other options — this can cost them
their current job and is a serious breach of trust `[KB-VREC-029]` (third-party, Holloway
back-channel reference guide, retrieved 2026-09-28). And do not treat the *absence* of an easily
reachable professional network as itself a red flag — this unfairly penalizes candidates who built
outside traditional, well-networked circles and is not evidence of anything about the person's
actual ability or trustworthiness `[KB-VREC-030]` (third-party, Holloway back-channel reference
guide, retrieved 2026-09-28).

## 6. Red flags checklist

**In a candidate:**
- Wants the "co-founder" title with no real skin in the game — no equity-at-risk commitment, no
  reduced/deferred pay, wants founder status but employee-level risk.
- Will not discuss equity, vesting, or a written agreement openly and early — evasiveness here
  (as opposed to simply wanting to think it over) tends to predict evasiveness later when something
  actually goes wrong.
- Ghosts, goes silent, or is chronically unresponsive during a trial project, especially without
  proactively communicating about a missed deadline.
- Allergic to any legal paperwork — resists even a simple written trial-scope agreement or refuses
  to engage with the idea of a founder agreement/vesting schedule at all. A healthy candidate may
  push back on specific terms; refusing the entire concept of putting anything in writing is
  different and worse.
- Cannot describe a real past conflict or hard call, or describes only conflicts that were
  entirely someone else's fault.
- Wants to skip the trial project entirely and go straight to a formal commitment — read as
  excitement outrunning diligence, not as a compliment to how promising the fit feels.
- A back-channel reference surfaces a specific, credible, repeated concern (not a single isolated
  negative opinion, but a pattern).
- Cannot articulate what they specifically bring that you don't already have — redundant skillset
  dressed up as enthusiasm.

**In the search process itself:**
- Rushing to fill a co-founder slot "to check a box" rather than because the specific gap and fit
  are real. First Round Review's "The Founder Dating Playbook" makes the "don't rush" point directly `[KB-VREC-031]` (third-party, First Round Review "The
  Founder Dating Playbook," retrieved 2026-09-28, verbatim: "You don't want to rush into a pairing or stop your search prematurely."). Its companion piece, "Looking for Love in All The Wrong Places," separately argues
  against solo founding on its own merits (quoting Steve Blank: "a single founder is like one hand
  clapping") but does not itself use "anxiety about being solo" or "keep telling the story" language
  — treat the "don't rush" advice as sourced to the Founder Dating Playbook specifically, not to
  Looking for Love.
- Searching only one channel and concluding "there's no one out there" after a few weeks — the
  realistic timeline (§7) is months, and the channel mix in §1 is intentionally broad because no
  single channel reaches every kind of candidate.
- Skipping the trial project because the candidate "seems great" on paper or in conversation.
- Negotiating a specific equity number before a real trial has happened.
- Not disclosing existing obligations (investors, advisors, prior commitments — see §8) until after
  a candidate has already emotionally committed.

## 7. Realistic search timelines

Set expectations against real data, not against how fast it feels like it "should" happen.

- **A commonly-repeated figure for YC Co-Founder Matching:** roughly **3-6 months** from first login
  to signing a co-founder agreement, with about **30% of active users** finding a co-founder within a
  year `[KB-VREC-032]` UNVERIFIED — seeded, needs derivation (this figure could not be confirmed
  directly against YC's own "Does co-founder matching work?" article, which this pass read through
  its embedded data without finding either number; the figure appears in third-party aggregation as
  if it were YC's own reported statistic, but that attribution itself is unconfirmed — do not call it
  YC's "own reported" figure until it is independently confirmed on a YC page; re-verify against
  ycombinator.com/library before citing a precise percentage to a founder).
- **Individual published accounts vary widely:** one founder met roughly 40 potential co-founders
  over about 4.5 months before finding the right fit; another met over 100 across 4+ months
  `[KB-VREC-033]` (third-party, aggregated from published founder accounts — Medium/Substack
  self-published pieces, not a controlled study — retrieved 2026-09-28; directional only).
- **One detailed, publicly documented case (First Round Review):** the founder "dated" six
  different candidates over roughly a year, then spent four more months working with her eventual
  co-founder before formally committing `[KB-VREC-034]` (third-party, First Round Review "The
  Founder Dating Playbook," retrieved 2026-09-28).
- **General framing across multiple sources:** since a co-founder relationship runs far longer and
  is far higher-stakes than an executive hire, and executive searches commonly take three to six
  months, a co-founder search deserves at least that much care `[KB-VREC-035]` (third-party, First Round Review
  "The Founder Dating Playbook," quoting Alfred Lin on executive hires taking three to six months,
  retrieved 2026-09-28).

**Bottom line for planning purposes:** budget on the order of **3-6+ months** of active,
multi-channel search to find and properly vet (including a real trial project) a serious co-founder
candidate. A faster outcome is possible and does happen, but should be treated as fortunate, not as
the baseline to plan around — and a search that has run only a few weeks in a single channel has
not yet given the process a fair test.

## 8. Pitching honestly when the product is mostly already built

A founder who already has a mostly-built product is in a genuinely different (and generally
stronger) negotiating position than a founder with only an idea — but this comes with a specific
honesty obligation: a later joiner is not joining at day one, and both sides need to be clear-eyed
about what that means before anyone gets emotionally invested.

**Equity reality — point to the numbers file, state the principle here.** The mechanism (not a
specific ratio) is: the default advice for co-founders who join at the very same moment, with the
same risk and the same unbuilt idea, is a straight equal split, because over a 10+ year build the
small differences in who did what in week one wash out `[KB-VREC-036]` (official, YC Startup Library
"How to Split Equity Among Co-Founders," retrieved 2026-09-28). **This file previously stated a
2:1-to-5:1 ratio and "less than a founding 50/50 split is the correct frame" as if YC's own guidance
supported a smaller late-joiner share — it does not.** YC's companion piece explicitly names "joined
after fundraising" and "joined post-launch" as *bad* reasons for an unequal split (see
`equity-and-agreements.md` §1), which is exactly the scenario a founder pitching an already-built
product is in. **Do not cite a specific ratio, and do not tell a candidate up front that a
smaller-than-equal split is "normal and fair"** — that framing runs counter to YC's own stated
position, not in line with it. What this file recommends instead: be honest early that the founder
has already put in real work and taken real risk the new joiner did not share, name that as a real
input to the conversation, and let the actual number be worked out with the candidate and, ultimately,
counsel — see `equity-and-agreements.md` §1 for the full reasoning and its decision rules. Setting
expectations honestly and early, before a candidate has emotionally committed, still avoids the
single most common source of late-stage resentment in this exact scenario — the honesty is about the
founder's actual history and risk taken, not about pre-committing to a smaller split.

**Being honest about the state of the business.** State plainly, without inflating or hiding:
- **Revenue and users** — real numbers, not "we have some interest" or "basically ready." If there's
  no revenue yet, say so and say why, rather than implying traction that doesn't exist.
- **Technical debt and what "mostly built" actually means** — a technical candidate needs to know
  what they're inheriting: architectural shortcuts, untested paths, anything built fast that needs
  rework. Overselling "it's basically done" is one of the fastest ways to lose their trust once they
  look at the code.
- **What's genuinely still open** — the gap the co-founder fills should be real and substantial, not
  a token role. If the honest answer is "I need someone to execute what I've already fully decided,"
  that's a senior-employee role, not a co-founder role, and should be described that way.

**Existing obligations a new co-founder needs to know before joining.** Disclose, before any formal
commitment:
- Any existing investors (even a small friends-and-family round, an angel check, or a SAFE) and
  what rights or expectations come with that money.
- Any existing advisors with an equity grant or side letter.
- Any existing contractor or vendor agreements that carry IP assignment implications (did a prior
  freelance developer retain any rights to code still in the product?).
- Any prior co-founder or partner situation that ended, and why, if relevant to what the new
  co-founder is walking into (this is also exactly the kind of thing a back-channel reference on
  *you*, the founder, would surface — assume a serious candidate may be doing this same diligence in
  reverse).

**Avoiding overselling — the general discipline.** Everywhere this file discusses honesty, the
underlying rule is the same: state the actual, current, verifiable facts (revenue, user count, what
code exists, what obligations exist) and let the opportunity's real merits do the persuading. A
candidate serious enough to be worth recruiting will find any gap between the pitch and the reality
within the trial period at the latest — the only question is whether they find out from the founder
directly (which builds trust) or find out themselves (which reads as concealment even if nothing
was technically a lie).

## Decision rules (consolidated)

The full reasoning behind each of these lives in §4 (scorecards) and inline where cited; this list
is the quick-reference version for an agent applying them directly.

- IF a candidate refuses to discuss equity/vesting/a written agreement, THEN red flag — slow down,
  do not proceed to a formal offer until resolved (§4, §6).
- IF a trial reveals a missed deadline or below-scope delivery with no proactive communication,
  THEN weight it heavily — a trial is the best-case version of how someone shows up (§3, §4).
- IF skills are strong but conflict-handling or values alignment is weak/evasive, THEN slow down
  regardless of skills — the dominant predictor of partnership failure, and harder to fix later than
  a skills gap (§4).
- IF the only interested candidates want employee-plus-options rather than true co-founder status,
  THEN treat as real market information and evaluate whether that structure meets the underlying
  need, rather than holding out indefinitely for a title match (§4, §8).
- IF only technical (or only business) candidates surface, THEN widen the channel mix toward where
  the missing skillset congregates rather than concluding the person doesn't exist (§1, §4).
- IF the best candidate is in a different country, THEN separate the working-relationship question
  from the legal/tax/equity question, and route the second to an attorney and cross-border tax
  advisor before proceeding (§4).
- IF a back-channel reference surfaces a specific, repeated concern across independent sources,
  THEN treat it as a real signal — but a single vague/secondhand comment, or no reachable network at
  all, is not on its own disqualifying (§5).
- IF a real trial project has not yet happened, THEN do not finalize equity, title, or a formal
  agreement — a strong call or reference check is not a substitute for seeing the person work (§3).
- IF a candidate has not yet been told about existing investors, advisors, technical debt, or true
  revenue/user numbers, THEN that disclosure happens before any commitment is asked for (§8).

## Anti-patterns

- **Posting in one channel and waiting.** The candidate pool that actually fits (skills +
  complementary role + real interest + geography/timezone fit) is narrow enough that a single
  channel rarely produces it — work multiple channels from §1 concurrently.
- **Skipping the trial project because the person "seems great."** Every credible source converges
  on the same point: a real trial, not just conversation, is what actually predicts fit.
  Conversation alone selects for charisma and self-presentation, not for how someone actually
  behaves under real, if small, pressure.
- **Treating an unpaid trial as a free, risk-free way to "try before you buy."** It can carry real
  wage-and-hour legal exposure depending on how it's structured — this needs a real look before it
  starts, not after a dispute (see §3 and `equity-and-agreements.md`).
- **Negotiating equity before a trial, or as part of the first conversation.** Confirm you're in a
  plausible range early so neither side wastes time, but do not finalize a number until real working
  time together has happened.
- **Overselling the state of the product or business to close a promising candidate faster.** This
  reliably backfires once the candidate actually looks under the hood, and the trust cost of being
  caught oversimplifying the truth is far higher than the cost of an honest but less flattering
  pitch.
- **Rushing to fill the co-founder slot out of loneliness or urgency rather than fit.** A wrong
  co-founder is far more costly to the business than staying solo longer while the product and
  story keep improving in the meantime.
- **Assuming the only valid outcome is a 50/50 equal-founder split.** For a product that's already
  mostly built, a smaller share for a later joiner is normal and expected — refusing to consider
  anything but an equal split (in either direction — the existing founder insisting on keeping too
  much, or a candidate insisting on full parity despite joining after most of the risk is retired)
  stalls otherwise-good matches.
- **Treating one bad reference, or no reachable references at all, as automatically disqualifying.**
  Context and pattern matter more than a single data point either way.
- **Skipping the back-channel step entirely and relying only on references the candidate hand-picked.**
  Candidate-supplied references are useful but structurally biased toward the positive; they are a
  floor, not a full check.
